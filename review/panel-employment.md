## CORRECTIONS REQUIRED

### [unpaid#02]

- WRONG: "If you worked through a platform, the written service agreement, the minimum notice before deactivation, conciliation and a dedicated Tribunal are now statutory rather than goodwill."
- WHY: Three errors. First, coverage is not platform-only. Section 2 of Act 872 defines a gig worker as an individual who "(b) enters into a service agreement with a contracting entity for the performance of— (i) any service with any contracting entity who is a platform provider; **or (ii) any service as specified in the Schedule with any contracting entity who is not a platform provider**". The Schedule names, at items 1, 2, 3 and 9, "Acting" (actor), "Filming activities" (film production crew), "Music-related activities" (singer, musician, composer, lyricist) and "Photography and videography" (photographer, videographer). A singer engaged directly by a promoter, with no platform anywhere, is a gig worker. Second, the deactivation protection is not general: s.14 imposes it on "a platform provider" only, and Part VIII social security likewise applies only "to a contracting entity who is a platform provider" (s.81). Third, "now statutory" is undated and the as-at date on this claim is the gazette date, not commencement. Section 1(2) provides that the Act "comes into operation on a date to be appointed by the Minister"; that date was 31 March 2026, with the Tribunal and Consultative Council stood up from 31 March and 1 April 2026 respectively. The gig worker must also be "a citizen or a permanent resident of Malaysia".
- REPLACE WITH: "Since 31 March 2026 the Gig Workers Act 2025 gives you a written service agreement, a right to be paid within seven days where the agreement is silent, conciliation and a dedicated Tribunal. It reaches you two ways: any service you provide through a platform, or, even with no platform involved, any service listed in the Schedule; acting, film production crew, singing, musicianship, composing, lyric writing, make-up, hair, styling, photography and videography are all listed by name. The deactivation notice rules and the PERKESO contribution apply only where the counterparty is a platform provider. You must be a Malaysian citizen or permanent resident."

### [stage-show#07]

- WRONG: "If the engagement runs through a platform, the service agreement, the notice before deactivation, conciliation and the Tribunal are now statutory."
- WHY: Same defect as unpaid#02 and it matters more here, because a stage performer is precisely who the Schedule captures. Schedule item 1 is "Acting / Actor" and item 3 is "Music-related activities / (a) singer (b) musician (c) composer (d) lyricist". Under s.2(b)(ii) those people are gig workers when the contracting entity is not a platform provider. Telling a performer the Act only bites "if the engagement runs through a platform" will cause them to walk away from a statutory route they have. The producer's side is also understated: "contracting entity" under s.2 includes "(a) an individual", so a sole-trader producer is bound by ss.3, 8, 9, 11 and 12.
- REPLACE WITH: "Since 31 March 2026 the Gig Workers Act 2025 covers actors, singers, musicians, composers and lyricists by name in its Schedule, whether or not a platform is involved. That means a written service agreement setting out the service, the rate and the payment method, payment within seven days where the agreement says nothing, no termination without just cause or excuse, conciliation before the Director General for Industrial Relations, and the Gig Workers Tribunal. A producer engaging them is a contracting entity even if he is an individual."

### [management-deal#08]

- WRONG: "If the counterparty is a platform, the new statutory protections may reach you. How 'platform' and 'service agreement' are read is exactly what ALIM should be pushing on."
- WHY: This frames as an open question something Parliament has already decided, and points ALIM's advocacy at the wrong target. Actors, film crew, singers, musicians, composers, lyricists, make-up artists, hair stylists, stylists, interpreters, translators, transcribers, journalists, stringers, photographers and videographers are already in the Act 872 Schedule, so no argument about the meaning of "platform" is needed for them. The genuine gap is who is missing from a closed list: dancers, choreographers, visual artists, writers and authors, theatre directors, stage and technical crew outside film, DJs and curators appear nowhere in it. Section 110 is the lever: "The Minister may, by order published in the Gazette, amend the Schedule." Note also that "service agreement" is defined to exclude a contract of service under the Employment Act 1955, the Labour Ordinance of Sabah, the Labour Ordinance of Sarawak and the Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994, and a contract of employment under the Industrial Relations Act 1967; so gig worker and employee are mutually exclusive, and the prior question is always which one you were.
- REPLACE WITH: "The Gig Workers Act 2025 already names actors, film crew, singers, musicians, composers, lyricists, make-up artists, stylists, photographers and videographers in its Schedule, so those artists are covered whether or not a platform is involved. Dancers, visual artists, writers, theatre directors, stage crew and DJs are not in the Schedule, and s.110 lets the Minister add them by order. That is where ALIM should be pushing. Note too that a gig worker cannot also be an employee; the Act's definition of service agreement expressly excludes a contract of service or of employment."

### [brand-deal#09]

- WRONG: "If the counterparty is a platform rather than a brand, the gig worker protections may reach the relationship."
- WHY: Wrong on the coverage test, for the reasons above. A creator shooting content is very likely a "photographer" or "videographer" under Schedule item 9, or crew under item 2, in which case the brand itself is a contracting entity under s.2 and the protections apply directly. Section 2 defines "contracting entity" as "(a) an individual; (b) any person including a body of persons incorporated or registered under any written law; or (c) any platform provider, who engages and enters into a service agreement with a gig worker". A brand is squarely within (b).
- REPLACE WITH: "If you are shooting stills or video, Schedule item 9 of the Gig Workers Act 2025 lists photographers and videographers, so the brand itself is a contracting entity and the protections apply directly; you do not need a platform in the chain. That gives you a written service agreement, payment within seven days where the agreement is silent, no deduction from your fee except in the narrow cases s.12 allows, and conciliation followed by the Gig Workers Tribunal."

### [unpaid#04]

- WRONG: "For dismissal rather than unpaid wages: s.20, the Industrial Court, and sixty days from dismissal to file. Miss the clock and the claim is gone."
- WHY: You do not file at the Industrial Court, and an artist who tries to will lose the sixty days in the wrong building. Section 20(1) of Act 177 reads: "Where a workman ... considers that he has been dismissed without just cause or excuse by his employer, he may make representations in writing **to the Director General** to be reinstated in his former employment; the representations may be filed at the office of the Director General nearest to the place of employment from which the workman was dismissed." Section 20(1A): "The Director General shall not entertain any representations under subsection (1) unless such representations are filed within sixty days of the dismissal", with a proviso extending time where the workman is dismissed with notice. Reference to the Industrial Court is not something the worker does; under s.20(3), as amended by Act A1615 with effect from 1 January 2021, "the Director General **shall** refer the representations to the Court for an award" where he is satisfied there is no likelihood of settlement. The Minister's old discretion is gone.
- REPLACE WITH: "For dismissal rather than unpaid wages the route is s.20 of the Industrial Relations Act 1967. You file written representations with the Director General for Industrial Relations, at the Industrial Relations Department office nearest the place you worked, within sixty days of the dismissal. You do not file at the Industrial Court. If conciliation fails the Director General must refer the case to the Industrial Court himself; since 1 January 2021 that is no longer a Ministerial discretion. Miss the sixty days and the Director General cannot entertain you at all."

### [management-deal#07]

- WRONG: "If you were in truth an employee rather than a contracting artist, s.20 is the route and the clock is sixty days from dismissal."
- WHY: Correct on the section and the period, but silent on where the representations go, which is the step artists get wrong. See s.20(1) and s.20(1A) quoted above. One addition worth making here: s.2 of Act 177 defines "workman" as "any person, including an apprentice, employed by an employer under a contract of employment to work for hire or reward", with no wage ceiling and no territorial limit, so a manager-artist relationship that is in truth employment reaches s.20 anywhere in Malaysia, including Sabah and Sarawak where the Employment Act 1955 does not run.
- REPLACE WITH: "If you were in truth an employee rather than a contracting artist, s.20 of the Industrial Relations Act 1967 is the route: written representations to the Director General for Industrial Relations, not to the Industrial Court, within sixty days of the dismissal. There is no wage ceiling on who counts as a workman, and this route runs throughout Malaysia."

### [unpaid#03]

- WRONG: "If you were in truth an employee, the Labour Department route replaces suing, and it is free."
- WHY: Two problems. First, s.69 of Act 265 does not replace a civil suit; nothing in the Act ousts the civil courts, and the employee may sue on the contract instead. Section 69(1) gives the Director General power to "inquire into and decide any dispute between an employee and his employer in respect of wages or any other payments in cash", and to order payment "of such sum of money as he deems just **without limitation of the amount thereof**". It is an alternative forum, and a better one, not an exclusive one. Second, and more dangerous, s.1(2) provides that "This Act shall apply to **Peninsular Malaysia only**", extended to Labuan by P.U. (A) 400/2000. An artist in Sabah or Sarawak has no Employment Act 1955 route. They go to the Labour Department under the Labour Ordinance of Sabah (Cap. 67) or the Labour Ordinance of Sarawak (Cap. 76), both amended by Acts A1753 and A1754 with effect from 1 May 2025.
- REPLACE WITH: "If you were in truth an employee, the Labour Department is the cheap route: the Director General can hear a wages dispute under s.69 of the Employment Act 1955 and order payment with no cap on the amount. It is an alternative to suing, not a bar on it, so you keep the civil claim if you would rather have it. In Sabah and Sarawak the Employment Act does not apply; the equivalent route is the Labour Department under the Labour Ordinance of Sabah or of Sarawak. Labelling you a freelancer does not make you one."

### [run-organisation#06]

- WRONG: "As an employer: notice, leave, the 2023 coverage of all employees, and the anti-discrimination provisions."
- WHY: The coverage statement is right in substance but the mechanism is not stated and the territorial limit is missing, and both change what an officer must actually do. ### [documentary#09]

- WRONG: "A set is a workplace, and since 2022 the duty reaches the principal, not just the direct employer of the crew."
- WHY: The date is wrong by two years and the duty is narrower than stated. Section 18A of Act 514, "Duties of principal", was inserted by the Occupational Safety and Health (Amendment) Act 2022 [Act A1648], which "came into operation on ... 1 June 2024" according to the AGC updated text hosted in this library. Nothing reached the principal in 2022 or 2023. Section 18A(2) then limits it: "The duty imposed on the principal in subsection (1) shall only apply where the contractor, subcontractor or employee referred to in that subsection is working under the direction of the principal as to the manner in which the work is carried out." The unqualified duty is the new s.18B: "Every employer, self-employed person or principal shall conduct a risk assessment in relation to the safety and health risk posed to any person who may be affected by his undertaking at the place of work."
- REPLACE WITH: "A set is a workplace. Since 1 June 2024, when the 2022 amendments came into force, the duty can reach the principal and not just the crew's direct employer, but only where the principal directs the manner in which the work is carried out. Independent of that, every employer, self-employed person and principal must now carry out a risk assessment for the place of work and act on it."

### [dj-club-night#11]

- WRONG: "A venue and a rig are a workplace. The 2022 amendments reach the principal, so a promoter cannot subcontract the safety risk down to the crew's own employer."
- WHY: "Cannot subcontract" is too absolute, and the amendments did not reach anyone until 1 June 2024. Section 18A(2) applies the principal's duty "only ... where the contractor, subcontractor or employee ... is working under the direction of the principal as to the manner in which the work is carried out". A promoter who hires a production company and leaves the method to it may be outside s.18A. He is not outside s.18B, which binds "every employer, self-employed person or principal" to assess and control risk without that qualification, nor outside s.17, which owes a duty to persons other than employees.
- REPLACE WITH: "A venue and a rig are a workplace. Since 1 June 2024 the 2022 amendments can put the duty on you as principal, so long as you are directing how the work is done; leaving the method entirely to the crew's own employer is what takes you outside that particular duty. It does not take you outside the risk assessment duty, which binds every principal, or the duty you owe to people who are not your employees."

### [stage-show#09]

- WRONG: "The reporting duties that apply if a child on the production is at risk."
- WHY: A producer has no reporting duty under the Child Act 2001. The mandatory duties are closed and personal: s.27(1) binds "a medical officer or a registered medical practitioner", s.28(1) binds "any member of the family of a child", and s.29(1) binds "a child care provider", defined in s.2 as "a person who looks after one or more children for valuable consideration for any period of time". A producer is none of those; a paid chaperone or tutor engaged by the production can be the third. What actually exposes a producer is s.31(1), which makes it an offence for "any person who, being a person having the care of a child" to abuse, neglect, abandon or expose the child, or to cause or permit that, punishable by a fine up to RM50,000 or twenty years' imprisonment or both. The Act applies throughout Malaysia under s.1(2).
- REPLACE WITH: "The Child Act 2001 does not make a producer a mandatory reporter; that duty sits on doctors, family members and paid child care providers, and a chaperone or tutor you engage and pay is a child care provider. What reaches the producer is s.31: it is an offence for a person having the care of a child to neglect or abuse the child, or to permit it, with up to twenty years' imprisonment. Appoint a named, paid chaperone and record who had care of the child at every point."

## FLAGGED BUT DEFENSIBLE

### [stage-show#06]

- WRONG: "control, integration and mutuality decide it"
- RISK: "Mutuality of obligation" is an English test. The Malaysian authority is the multiple or mixed test with control as the primary but not sole factor, and importing mutuality as a decisive element gives producers an argument that the resource itself has handed them. The Employment Act's own presumption is also narrower than a reader will assume: s.101C(1) lists control of the manner of work, control of hours, provision of tools, work forming an integral part of another's business, work performed solely for another's benefit, and regular payment constituting the majority of income, but it opens "In any proceeding for an offence under this Act, in the absence of a written contract of service", so it does not run in a s.69 wages claim or a s.20 dismissal claim. The rest of the claim is sound.
- REPLACE WITH: "Whether the cast and crew are employees decides notice, leave, sick pay and which forum hears a complaint. Calling someone a freelancer does not make them one. The courts look at the whole relationship, with control over how the work is done as the primary factor, alongside who provides the tools, whether the work is an integral part of the business, and how payment is made."

### [stage-show#08]

- WRONG: "Employment connected with public entertainment is separately regulated where a child is involved, and a parental release is not the whole answer."
- RISK: Accurate but so thin it does not tell a producer what to obtain, and it omits the territorial limit and the scope of "take part". Section 7(1) of Act 350 is the operative provision: "No child or young person shall take part or be required or permitted to take part in any public entertainment unless there has been issued by the Director General of Labour ... to the person employing such child or young person a licence in that behalf". The licence is issued to the employer, not the parent, and the Director General must refuse it where the employment "is dangerous to the life, limb, health, safety or morals" of the child (s.7(2)). Section 1A defines a person as taking part in an entertainment "when such person is employed in or connected with such entertainment whether as a performer, stage-hand or musician", so a child stagehand needs the licence too. The Third Schedule regulations add a Government Medical Officer examination in the first instance and at least once every three months, and cap any single performance at four hours. Section 1(2) confines the Act to Peninsular Malaysia.
- REPLACE WITH: "Where a child or young person takes part in public entertainment you need a licence under s.7 of the Children and Young Persons (Employment) Act 1966, issued by the Director General of Labour to you as the employer. A parental release is not that licence. It covers a child working as a stagehand or musician, not just on stage, and it carries conditions: a medical examination at the outset and every three months, and no performance longer than four hours. The Director General must refuse a licence where the work is dangerous to the child's life, limb, health, safety or morals. In Sabah and Sarawak the equivalent rules are in the respective Labour Ordinance."

### [dj-club-night#12], [stage-show#11], [run-organisation#08]

## MATERIAL OMISSIONS

1. **Self-Employment Social Security Act 2017 (Act 789) is hosted in the library and cited by no claim, and it is the single most useful thing a freelance artist could be told.** Section 2 applies the Act to "any citizen or permanent resident of Malaysia who is carrying out a self-employment activity in the industry specified in the First Schedule". First Schedule item 18 reads: "(a) The services in any performance, film activities and any activity which includes artistic work and graphic work", with "performance", "artistic work" and "graphic work" taking their Copyright Act 1987 meanings and "film activities" its FINAS Act 1981 meaning. Performers, film workers and visual artists are therefore a named self-employment sector, not an arguable one. Section 11(1) is mandatory in terms: "Every self-employed person shall register with and pay contribution to the Organization ... for every self-employment activity he is carrying out", and s.11(4) makes non-compliance an offence carrying a fine up to RM10,000 or two years' imprisonment or both. The scheme buys medical benefit, temporary and permanent disablement benefit, dependants' benefit and funeral benefit for a self-employed artist who has no employer and no SOCSO. Nothing in the resource mentions it. A freelance artist injured on a get-in with no cover is the exact person this resource is written for.

2. **Employees Provident Fund Act 1991 (Act 452) and Employees' Social Security Act 1969 (Act 4) are hosted and cited by no claim.** If the producer's conclusion under stage-show#06 is that cast or crew are employees, the consequence is not only notice and leave; it is mandatory EPF registration and monthly contributions, and mandatory SOCSO registration and contributions, both with arrears, interest and personal liability for directors and partners. The Employment Injury Scheme also covers accidents travelling to and from the place of work, which is a live risk on a touring or location production. A resource that tells a producer employee status "decides notice, leave, sick pay and which forum hears a complaint" and stops there understates the bill by the largest item in it.

3. **Sexual harassment under the Employment Act 1955 Part XVA is not mentioned anywhere.** Section 81B(1) requires an employer to inquire into a complaint of sexual harassment on receipt, s.81B(2) requires him to give written reasons for any refusal, s.81F makes failure an offence, and s.81H requires a notice to be exhibited to raise awareness. This is the employment-side route that runs in parallel with the Act 840 Tribunal, and it is the one that binds a producer or an arts organisation as an employer. Act 840 gives the complainant a forum; Part XVA gives the employer a duty. The resource covers only the first.

4. **Territorial reach is nowhere stated, and two of the Acts in scope are Peninsular-only.** Act 265 s.1(2) and Act 350 s.1(2) both confine those Acts to Peninsular Malaysia, the former extended to Labuan. Sabah and Sarawak run on the Labour Ordinance of Sabah (Cap. 67) and the Labour Ordinance of Sarawak (Cap. 76), amended by Acts A1753 and A1754 in force from 1 May 2025, which harmonised coverage of all employees regardless of wage, 98 days' maternity leave, seven days' paternity leave and a 45-hour week. The outcome for a Kuching artist is broadly similar but the statute, the section numbers and the forum are different. Act 177, Act 611, Act 789 and Act 872 have no such limit. For a national arts resource this needs to be said once, prominently.

5. **No claim states a single employment entitlement in figures.** run-organisation#06 says "notice, leave" and stops. Verified from the hosted text and worth stating: maternity leave is "not less than ninety-eight consecutive days" under s.37(1)(d)(ii) of Act 265 as amended by Act A1651, available to every female employee; the separate entitlement to maternity allowance under s.37(2)(a) requires employment for at least ninety days in aggregate during the nine months immediately before confinement and employment at any time in the four months immediately before confinement. Terminating a female employee during her maternity leave is an offence under s.37(4). Paternity leave under s.60FA is seven consecutive days at the ordinary rate of pay, for a married male employee, limited to five confinements. The minimum wage has been RM1,700 a month since 1 February 2025 under the Minimum Wages Order 2024, and since 1 August 2025 without the small-employer grace period. Any of these will be the first thing an artist actually wants to know.

6. **Trade Unions Act 1959 (Act 262) is hosted and cited by no claim, and the union point matters differently now.** Act 872 s.10 makes void any term restricting a gig worker's right to form, join or take part in a gig workers' association, but s.2 defines that association as one "registered under the Societies Act 1966", not the Trade Unions Act. Artists organising as gig workers and artists organising as employees are on two separate statutory tracks, and the resource says nothing about either.

7. **No claim cites any subsidiary legislation under Act 872, and some now exists.** The Gig Workers Tribunal regulations and the Gig Workers (Compounding of Offences) Regulations 2026 [P.U. (A) 145/2026] were made around commencement on 30 and 31 March 2026. If a later draft cites regulations, cite the P.U. (A) numbers; I verified only that these two exist and did not verify a complete list.

8. **Act 872 does not establish a Gig Workers Commission, and any claim that says otherwise should be struck before it is written.** Act 872 establishes the Gig Workers Tribunal (Part V, s.24) and the Consultative Council (Part VI, s.46), whose 26 members were appointed with effect from 1 April 2026. The Malaysian Gig Economy Commission, SEGiM, exists but is an administrative body under the Prime Minister's Department, not a creature of Act 872. Press reporting conflates the two; the Act's own text does not.

9. **The Gig Workers Act route is sequential and a gig worker cannot go straight to the Tribunal.** Section 17(1) requires the complaint to go first to the contracting entity's internal grievance mechanism, which must resolve it within thirty days, and s.17(3) exempts deactivation disputes from that step. Section 18(1) makes the Director General for Industrial Relations and his officers the Conciliator. Section 33(1) then limits the Tribunal to "any dispute or matter referred to it by the Conciliator or the Minister", and s.33(2) excludes offences and all Part VIII matters from its jurisdiction. An artist who writes to the Tribunal first will be sent back. No claim sets out the sequence.
