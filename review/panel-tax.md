# ALIM legislation library; panel review
## Tax, corporate, commercial and data protection scope
Reviewer: senior Malaysian counsel (tax / corporate / commercial / data protection panel seat)
Date of review: 21 September 2026
Instruments in scope: Act 53, Act 807, Act 103, Act 378, Act 136, Act 137, Act 254, Act 646, Act 300, Act 599, Act 658, Act 709, Act 777, Act 832, Act 743, Act 135, Act 200, Act 155 + PUSPAL, Act 645, Act 629, Act 724, Act 653, Act 91, Act 67.

## VERDICT
PUBLISH AFTER CORRECTIONS. Sixteen corrections are required across the in-scope claims; none of them is fatal to the project, but four are the kind that make a reader worse off than if they had read nothing. In order of danger: the Contracts Act s.28 claims tell artists that exclusivity is weak when the leading Malaysian authority is a music case holding the opposite, which invites a reader to breach an enforceable in-term exclusive; two Stamp Act claims state the curability rule backwards and would make a claimant abandon a good case; [merch-online#01] instructs readers to comply with a revoked set of regulations; and [archive#04] applies the Distribution Act 1958 to estates it does not reach, including every Muslim estate and every Sabah estate. The s.109A withholding claims; the ones the panel was asked to test hardest; are substantially **right** on rate, mechanism, payer liability and the 10 per cent increase, and the corrections there are that "personally liable" is wrong for an incorporated promoter and that the stated exposure is roughly half the real one. Separately, four hosted PDFs are not fit to be cited: two have no text layer, one is Bahasa Malaysia only and thirteen years stale, and the PDPA text predates the amendments the resource tells readers about.

## CORRECTIONS REQUIRED

### [management-deal#03] and [unpaid#06]: Stamp Act curability stated backwards
- WRONG: "Section 52 shuts an unstamped instrument out of evidence, and it is only curable before you need the document." (management-deal#03) and "Curable, but only before you need it." (unpaid#06)
- WHY: This is the reverse of s.52(1) Stamp Act 1949. The proviso to s.52(1) says in terms: "any such instrument shall, subject to all just exceptions, be admitted in evidence on payment of the duty and the penalty, if any, chargeable in respect thereof under section 47A". The cure is expressly available **at the moment of tender**, and ss.51 and 53 build the machinery for it: the person receiving the evidence impounds the instrument, duty and penalty are paid, the Collector stamps it under s.47A and returns it. Telling an artist the defect is incurable once proceedings start is capable of making them abandon a good claim. It also contradicts the resource's own [sync-licence#04], which states the position correctly.
- REPLACE WITH: "Stamp it. Section 52 keeps an unstamped instrument out of evidence, but the proviso to s.52(1) lets the court admit it on payment of the duty and the s.47A penalty, and s.51 lets the document be impounded and stamped mid-case. So the defect is curable even at trial; it is just expensive, embarrassing and it hands the other side a delay. Stamp within thirty days of execution and none of this arises."

### [management-deal#03], [unpaid#06], [release-track#07], [sync-licence#04], [exhibit-sell-art#05], [publish-book#06], [someone-used-my-work#11]: s.47A penalty figures and the 2026 self-assessment regime are missing
- WRONG: No claim anywhere states what late stamping actually costs, and none mentions that the stamp duty self-assessment system (STSDS) began phasing in on 1 January 2026.
- WHY: Section 47A as consolidated at 1 January 2026 now reads: RM50 or 10 per cent of the deficient duty, whichever is greater, if stamped within three months after the time for stamping; RM100 or 20 per cent of the deficient duty, whichever is greater, in any other case. That is a two-tier penalty, not the old three-tier one. Separately, the Stamp Duty Self-Assessment System began on 1 January 2026. Phase 1 covers rental and lease instruments, general stamping (which is where most arts agreements sit) and securities-related instruments; Phase 2 takes transfers of property from 1 January 2027 and Phase 3 the remainder from 1 January 2028. That changes the artist's duty from "send it in and wait for the Collector to assess" to "assess it yourself through MyTax and expect to be audited". **Do not tell readers that 2026 is an amnesty.** LHDN's special 2026 waiver is narrow: it covers penalties arising from an incorrect stamp duty return, incorrect or inaccurate information affecting the assessment, and s.72D(2) offences picked up on audit, for Phase 1 instruments stamped between 1 January and 31 December 2026. It expressly does **not** cover late stamping or non-stamping, so a drawer of old unstamped agreements still attracts the full s.47A penalty.
- REPLACE WITH: "Stamp within thirty days of execution. Late stamping costs you the unpaid duty plus, under s.47A, RM50 or ten per cent of the deficient duty (whichever is greater) if you fix it within three months of the deadline, and RM100 or twenty per cent (whichever is greater) after that. Since 1 January 2026 most arts agreements fall in Phase 1 of the stamp duty self-assessment system, which means you assess and pay through MyTax yourself and can be audited on it afterwards. LHDN has waived certain 2026 penalties for getting the self-assessed return wrong, but that waiver does not touch late stamping, so an old unstamped agreement is still a full-penalty problem."

### [merch-online#01]: cites a revoked instrument as the operative law
- WRONG: "The Consumer Protection (Electronic Trade Transactions) Regulations 2012 require an online seller to display name, registration number and address."
- WHY: The 2012 Regulations were revoked. The Consumer Protection (Electronic Trade Transactions) Regulations 2024 came into force on 25 December 2024, with a six-month transition that expired on 24 June 2025. The 2024 Regulations are wider than the 2012 set: they impose disclosure duties on both the seller and the online marketplace operator, and Regulation 4 requires the prescribed information to be displayed in Bahasa Malaysia (other languages may be used in addition, not instead). An artist store that complies with the 2012 list and publishes only in English is not compliant. This is the scenario's own watch-claim, so the error sits on the single point the page tells the reader to act on.
- REPLACE WITH: "The Consumer Protection (Electronic Trade Transactions) Regulations 2024 replaced the 2012 Regulations on 25 December 2024, and the transition period ended on 24 June 2025. If you sell online you must display your name or business name, your registration number if you have one, your contact details including address, email and telephone, a description and the price of what you are selling, the payment methods, the estimated delivery terms and your returns and refund policy. The Regulations also require the information to be given in Bahasa Malaysia, with other languages allowed alongside it; KPDN suspended enforcement of that language rule in June 2025 and has not set a new date, but the rule itself has not been revoked. Most artist stores display none of this. Implied guarantees and the Tribunal for Consumer Claims apply either way, and the Tribunal can hear claims up to RM50,000."

### [someone-used-my-work#12]: s.29 Limitation Act stated without its condition
- WRONG: "Six years, and s.29 postpones time where the use was concealed."
- WHY: Section 29 does not postpone time where a use was merely concealed or merely undiscovered. On the statutory wording, time runs from discovery only where the action is based on the **fraud** of the defendant or his agent, or the right of action was **concealed by the fraud** of such a person, or the action is for relief from the consequences of a **mistake**. Ordinary ignorance that your work was being used is not enough; Malaysia has no discoverability rule for latent damage in these categories. Telling an artist that late discovery postpones time invites them to sit on a claim that is already statute-barred. The parallel claims at [management-deal#05] and [unpaid#07] do carry the word "fraud" and are sound; this one drops it.
- REPLACE WITH: "Six years from when the cause of action accrued, which for infringement is the date of the infringing act, not the date you found out. Section 29 only postpones time where the claim is based on the other side's fraud, where they concealed the claim by fraud, or where you are seeking relief from a mistake. Simply not knowing is not enough, so date-stamp your discovery and take advice early rather than assuming the clock has not started."

### [post-set-online#07], [documentary#04], [merch-online#07], [exhibit-sell-art#07], [brand-deal#08], [run-organisation#09], [archive#06]: PDPA: the DPO duty is threshold-based and will not reach an artist
- WRONG: "Breach notification and the data protection officer duty arrived with the 2024 amendments and are being enforced." (post-set-online#07) and the same duty asserted without qualification at [merch-online#07].
- WHY: Both duties commenced on 1 June 2025 under the phased commencement of the Personal Data Protection (Amendment) Act 2024, and the DPO duty is expressly threshold-based. Under the Commissioner's guidelines a controller or processor must appoint a DPO only where it processes the personal data of 20,000 or more data subjects, or the sensitive personal data of 10,000 or more data subjects, or where the core activity requires regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects. A creator filming a room, a gallery with a mailing list and a merch store will not meet any of those. Stating the DPO duty flatly to that readership tells people to hire or appoint someone they do not need. Breach notification, by contrast, has no threshold: it applies to any controller and requires notice to the Commissioner as soon as practicable and in any case within 72 hours **of the occurrence of the breach**, not of discovery, with notice to affected data subjects without unnecessary delay and within 7 days of notifying the Commissioner where the breach causes or is likely to cause significant harm.
- REPLACE WITH: "Filming the room means processing other people's personal data. Since 1 June 2025 you must notify the Commissioner of a personal data breach as soon as practicable and no later than 72 hours after it happens, and then tell the affected people within 7 days of notifying the Commissioner where the breach causes or is likely to cause them significant harm. Note the trigger: the 72 hours runs from the breach happening, not from you finding out about it, and if you miss it you have to give the Commissioner a written explanation with supporting evidence. There is no size threshold on that duty. The data protection officer duty is different: it only bites if you process the personal data of 20,000 or more people, the sensitive personal data of 10,000 or more, or your core activity is regular and systematic monitoring. Almost no individual artist will reach that."

### [dj-club-night#10], [release-track#06], [management-deal#01], [brand-deal#04]: s.28 Contracts Act: the resource gets restraint of trade backwards for in-term exclusivity
- WRONG: "Section 28 makes agreements in restraint of trade void, which is why most radius and exclusivity clauses in Malaysian booking agreements are weaker than they read." (dj-club-night#10); "Section 28 makes restraint of trade void, which weakens most exclusivity." (release-track#06); "which is why most exclusivity and post-term commission clauses in Malaysian artist agreements are weaker than they look." (management-deal#01); "Section 28 again on category exclusivity." (brand-deal#04)
- WHY: This is the most operationally dangerous statement in the commercial scope, because unlike the tax claims it invites the reader to act. Section 28 reads: "Every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind, is to that extent void", with only three exceptions (sale of goodwill; partners on or in anticipation of dissolution; partners during the partnership). The leading Malaysian authority is itself a music case: **Polygram Records Sdn Bhd v Hillary Ang & Ors (The Search) [1994] 3 MLJ 127**, where the High Court held that s.28 is directed at restraints operating **after** the contract ends, and that a covenant giving the record company exclusive recording rights **during the currency of the agreement** is not a covenant in restraint of trade and is not void under s.28. An artist who reads this page, concludes their exclusive is "weaker than it reads" and records or plays for someone else mid-term is in plain breach and exposed to an injunction and damages. Separately, a post-term commission or sunset clause is a payment obligation, not a restraint on exercising a profession, so s.28 is not obviously engaged by it either. What s.28 does strike down, and strikes down hard, is the post-term restraint: Malaysian courts have no discretion to read a restraint down for reasonableness the way English courts do (see Nagadevan a/l Mahalingam v Millennium Medicare Services [2011] 4 MLJ 739), so a post-term radius, non-compete or exclusivity clause is void outright unless it falls in one of the three exceptions.
- REPLACE WITH (dj-club-night#10): "The booking is a contract whether or not anyone signed anything. Section 28 makes agreements in restraint of trade void, and Malaysian courts do not soften a restraint for reasonableness the way English courts do; if it is a restraint it is simply void. But the section bites on what happens **after** the deal ends. A radius clause that stops you playing another room in the weeks after this show is squarely in its sights. An exclusivity that only binds you while the booking is on foot is not: in Polygram Records v Hillary Ang the High Court upheld exactly that kind of in-term exclusive against a band. Treat the post-term restriction as the weak one and the in-term one as real."
- REPLACE WITH (management-deal#01): "Section 28 makes agreements in restraint of trade void, and there is no reasonableness discretion in Malaysia; a restraint is either outside the section or it is void. The section reaches post-term restrictions, so a clause stopping you signing elsewhere for two years after the deal ends is vulnerable. It does not reach exclusivity that only operates while the deal is running: Polygram Records v Hillary Ang upheld an in-term exclusive recording covenant against a band. A post-term commission on income from deals done during the term is a payment obligation rather than a restraint, so do not assume s.28 kills it; negotiate a sunset instead. Sections 15, 16 and 20 cover coercion, undue influence and mistake if you need to set the contract aside."
- REPLACE WITH (brand-deal#04): "Usage period, exclusivity, approvals, kill fee and whitelisting. Category exclusivity that runs only for the campaign term is ordinarily enforceable; it is the tail after the campaign ends that s.28 of the Contracts Act attacks, so price the tail or cut it."

### [stage-show#05]: Entertainments Duty exemptions described as practice, when they are statutory
- WRONG: "Entertainments duty on paid admissions, administered by the state with exemption practice that varies."
- WHY: The exemptions are in the Act, not in practice. Section 4(2) of the Entertainments Duty Act 1953 charges duty at **half** the prescribed rate on admission to an entertainment where all the performers are actually present and performing and the entertainment consists solely of a stage play, a ballet, a performance of music (vocal or instrumental), a lecture, a recitation, a music hall or other variety entertainment, a circus, a travelling show, a menagerie or a game of sport. Section 4(3)(a) exempts admission entirely for a stage play, a ballet or traditional dancing, a performance of music, a lecture or a recitation where the Collector is satisfied the entertainment is provided by a society, institution or committee **not conducted or established for profit**, or that the entertainment is **amateur**. Section 4(3)(b) then defines away amateur status if any performer, instructor, producer, manager, conductor or adviser is paid. A producer told the exemption is variable practice will not go looking for it; a producer told the half-rate and the non-profit route will.
- REPLACE WITH: "Entertainments duty is charged on payments for admission. Two reliefs in the Act itself are worth knowing: s.4(2) halves the rate where all the performers are actually present and performing and the bill is a stage play, ballet, music performance, lecture, recitation, variety, circus, travelling show or sport; and s.4(3)(a) exempts a stage play, ballet or traditional dance, music performance, lecture or recitation altogether where the Collector is satisfied it is put on by a non-profit society, institution or committee or that it is amateur. Note s.4(3)(b): pay any performer, instructor, producer, manager, conductor or adviser and it stops being amateur. Check which enactment applies to your state, because the collecting regime outside the Federal Territories is a state one."

### [dj-club-night#06]: "administered by the state" is loose about Act 103
- WRONG: "Entertainments duty is charged on paid admissions and administered by the state."
- WHY: Under the Act the resource links to, the Collector is the **Minister of Finance** (s.3(1)) and the rate is set by resolution of the Dewan Rakyat (s.4(1)). Act 103 is a federal instrument. Entertainment duty is administered at state level outside the Federal Territories under separate state enactments, which is a different body of law from the one the page cites. As written the reader is pointed at a federal Act and told a state administers it, which is how promoters end up asking the wrong office.
- REPLACE WITH: "Entertainments duty is charged on payments for admission. In the Federal Territories it runs under the Entertainments Duty Act 1953, where the Collector is the Minister of Finance and the rate is set by resolution of the Dewan Rakyat. Outside the Federal Territories each state has its own entertainment duty enactment and its own rate and exemptions, so confirm which one you are under before you price the ticket. Either way it is a real line on a ticketed show's budget and first-time promoters leave it out."

### [dj-club-night#07], [foreign-artist#03]: s.109A: right on rate and mechanism, wrong to say "personally liable" without qualification, and it under-states the cost
- WRONG: "Failing to deduct makes you personally liable for the tax plus a 10 per cent increase." (dj-club-night#07) and "Fail to deduct and you are personally liable for the tax plus a 10 per cent increase." (foreign-artist#03)
- WHY, on the parts that are right: the rate, the mechanism and the increase all check out against the hosted text as at 1 January 2026. Schedule 1, Part II, paragraph 3 charges "Income of a non-resident person (other than a company) consisting of remuneration or other income in respect of services performed or rendered in Malaysia by a public entertainer" at **15% of gross**. Section 109A provides that "The provisions of sections 109 and 110 shall apply mutatis mutandis to remuneration or other income in respect of services performed or rendered in Malaysia by a public entertainer", which is how the payer's machinery in s.109 is imported. Section 109(1) puts the duty on the payer to deduct on paying or crediting and to render an account and pay it over **within one month**. Section 109(2) provides that where the payer fails to pay, "that amount which he fails to pay shall be increased by a sum equal to ten per cent of the amount which he fails to pay, and that amount and the increased sum shall be a debt due from him to the Government and shall be payable forthwith". The s.2 definition of "public entertainer" is wide enough to catch a DJ: it includes "a compere, model, circus performer, lecturer, speaker, sportsperson, an artiste or individual exercising any profession, vocation or employment of a similar nature" and "an individual who uses his intellectual, artistic, musical, personal or physical skill or character in" carrying out any activity through live or other medium.
- WHY the correction is needed: "personally liable" is right for a sole-trader promoter and for a partnership (s.109(4) provides that "person" includes a partnership), but it is wrong for the very common case where the promoter is a sendirian berhad. There the s.109(2) debt is the **company's**. A director reaches personal liability only through s.75A, which makes a director jointly and severally liable for tax due from a company, and only a director who, alone or with associates, controls not less than twenty per cent of the ordinary share capital. Telling an incorporated promoter they are personally liable is wrong in law; telling them their company is liable and that a twenty per cent director gets pulled in under s.75A is both correct and more frightening, because most small promoter companies have exactly that shareholder.
- REPLACE WITH: "Book a non-resident DJ and the withholding under s.109A is yours, not the artist's. The rate is 15 per cent of the gross under Part II of Schedule 1, you deduct on paying or crediting and you must account for it and pay it to the Director General within one month. Miss it and s.109(2) adds a further 10 per cent and makes the whole thing a debt payable forthwith. If you book through a company the debt is the company's, but s.75A makes any director who controls twenty per cent or more of the shares jointly and severally liable for it, which in a two-person promoter company is everybody."

### [dj-club-night#07], [foreign-artist#03], [release-track#08], [sync-licence#05]: the real cost of not withholding is missing: s.39(1)(q) kills the deduction
- WRONG: The resource stops at "the tax plus a 10 per cent increase". That is not the whole bill.
- WHY: Section 39(1)(q) of the Income Tax Act disallows, in computing adjusted income, "any remuneration or other income in respect of services performed or rendered in Malaysia by a public entertainer from which tax is deductible under section 109A, if tax has not been deducted therefrom and paid to the Director General in accordance with that section". The proviso restores the deduction only if the payer has paid the tax **and** the increased sum under s.109(2), and paying late can still attract a penalty under s.113(2). So on a RM100,000 fee the exposure is not RM15,000 plus RM1,500; it is RM15,000 plus RM1,500 plus the loss of a RM100,000 deduction, which at the 24 per cent company rate is another RM24,000 of tax. Section 109 also has an equivalent disallowance route for royalties. The one piece of good news the resource also omits is s.109(3A), which lets the Director General remit the whole or part of the increase for good cause shown.
- REPLACE WITH (append to the s.109A claims): "The increase is not the worst of it. Section 39(1)(q) disallows the whole fee as a deduction if the tax was not withheld and paid over, and you only get the deduction back by paying the tax and the ten per cent increase. On a six-figure fee that turns a five-figure problem into a six-figure one. The Director General can remit the increase for good cause under s.109(3A), but do not plan on it."

### [foreign-artist#01], [foreign-artist#03], [dj-club-night#09]: PUSPAL and the withholding are not two separate problems; the tax is a gate on the clearance
- WRONG: The resource treats PUSPAL clearance and s.109A withholding as two parallel items on a checklist. [foreign-artist#01] says clearance comes before the pass; [foreign-artist#03] says the tax is the payer's liability. Neither says they are linked.
- WHY: On the Ministry of Communications' own PUSPAL portal the sequence is: apply through the ePUSPAL system, and after approval **complete the withholding tax payment** and then attend the PUSPAL counter to collect the approval letter. The approval letter is what the Immigration Department wants before it will issue the Visit Pass (Professional), and the applicant must be outside Malaysia when the pass application is made, entering on an entry visa. So the tax is not a year-end reconciliation item; it is a pre-event gate, and a promoter who has not budgeted for it does not get the letter, does not get the pass and does not get the show. This is the single most useful practical point available on this scenario and it is the one the scenario's own watch-claim is reaching for.
- REPLACE WITH (foreign-artist#01): "PUSPAL clearance comes before the immigration pass, and it is administered by the Ministry of Communications through the ePUSPAL system. Two things about the sequence catch promoters out. First, PUSPAL will not release the approval letter until the withholding tax on the artist's fee has been paid, so the tax is a gate on the clearance, not a problem for later. Second, its dress and conduct conditions are a live artistic freedom issue that appears in no statute and changes without any amendment being gazetted, so check the current guidance rather than last year's."

### [foreign-artist#03], [dj-club-night#07]: "the fee" is not the base; benefits in kind are inside the gross
- WRONG: The claims speak only of "payments" and "the fee", and the scenario lede tells the promoter to "budget the withholding into the fee".
- WHY: The charge in Schedule 1 Part II is on **gross**, and LHDN's Public Ruling No. 6/2017 on withholding tax on the income of a non-resident public entertainer treats both monetary and non-monetary income as within it: fees, per diems, appearance fees, a share of gate receipts and image-rights fees on the monetary side; accommodation, meals, airfare, laundry, ground transport and any tax borne by the sponsor on the non-monetary side. A promoter who withholds 15 per cent of the artist fee and separately pays business-class flights and a suite has under-withheld, and the shortfall carries the s.109(2) increase and the s.39(1)(q) disallowance. Note also that a fee grossed up so the artist receives a net figure makes the tax borne by the promoter part of the gross, which increases the base again.
- REPLACE WITH: "Section 109A withholding on payments to non-resident public entertainers is the payer's liability at 15 per cent of the **gross**, and gross means more than the fee. LHDN treats per diems, appearance fees, a share of the door and image-rights money as part of it, and also the non-cash side: flights, hotel, meals, ground transport and any tax you agree to bear. Agree a net fee and the tax you absorb is itself part of the gross, so the real number is higher again. Budget it from the first email; renegotiating 15 per cent after the artist has agreed a net figure is where these deals die."

### [foreign-artist#03]: the 15 per cent does not simply attach to a foreign agency's invoice
- WRONG: The claim reads as though every payment out on a foreign booking is a 15 per cent s.109A payment.
- WHY: Paragraph 3 of Part II of Schedule 1 charges the income of "a non-resident person (**other than a company**)". Where the promoter contracts with and pays a foreign agency, management company or loan-out vehicle rather than the individual, the invoice total is not automatically a s.109A payment. The entertainer's remuneration element remains within s.109A, while the balance retained by the company is a different analysis and may fall to be considered under s.4A and s.109B at a different rate. I have not resolved that split for this review and the resource should not assert one either. What it must not do is leave a promoter thinking that withholding 15 per cent of an agency invoice discharges the obligation.
- REPLACE WITH (add as a second sentence): "If you are paying a foreign agency, management company or loan-out rather than the artist personally, do not assume 15 per cent of the invoice total is the answer. The entertainer's remuneration and the company's own slice are analysed differently and a different withholding provision can apply to the balance. Get that split advised before you pay, because the cost of getting it wrong is the tax, the ten per cent increase and the loss of the deduction."

### [archive#04]: Distribution Act 1958 does not reach Sabah, does not reach Muslims, and the claim says neither
- WRONG: "When an artist dies intestate, this Act decides who inherits the catalogue, and therefore who can now give you permission."
- WHY: Two carve-outs on the face of the Act. Section 1(2): "This Act shall apply to Peninsular Malaysia only." It was extended to Sarawak by the Modification of Laws (Distribution Act 1958) (Extension to the State of Sarawak) Order 1986, P.U. (A) 446/1986. **Sabah is not covered**; intestacy there runs under Sabah's own succession law. Section 2: "Nothing in this Act shall apply to the estate of any person professing the Muslim religion or shall affect any rules of Muslim law as varied by local custom in respect of the distribution of the estate of any such person", and it also excludes Parsee intestate succession. A Muslim artist's catalogue devolves under faraid, administered through the Syariah court with a sijil faraid, and the person who can give an archivist permission is identified by a completely different route. For a resource serving Malaysian artists, an intestacy claim that mentions neither the Sabah gap nor faraid is not usable.
- REPLACE WITH: "When a non-Muslim artist dies intestate in Peninsular Malaysia or Sarawak, the Distribution Act 1958 decides who inherits the catalogue and therefore who can now give you permission; s.6 sets the shares between spouse, issue and parents. The Act does not apply in Sabah, which has its own succession law, and by s.2 it does not apply to the estate of a Muslim at all. A Muslim artist's estate is distributed under faraid, and the people who can licence the work are those named in the sijil faraid issued by the Syariah court. Establish which regime you are in before you write to anyone."

### [run-organisation#05]: House to House and Street Collections Act 1947 does not reach a benefit gig
- WRONG: "Public fundraising, street collections and benefit gigs need a licence."
- WHY: Section 2 defines "collection" as "an appeal to the public or any class of the public, made by means of visits from house to house or of soliciting in streets or other public places, or by both such means, to give, whether for consideration or not, money or other property". That is a door-to-door or street appeal. A ticketed benefit gig is a sale of admission, not a collection, and online crowdfunding is not a house-to-house or street appeal either. Telling an arts NGO that a benefit gig needs a licence sends it to apply for something it does not need, and it buries the thing that **is** caught: passing a bucket in the street or at a public place, and door-knocking. The licensing officer is the Chief Police Officer for the area, or the Inspector-General for the whole of Peninsular Malaysia, with the Chief Minister or the Minister as "the authority" who may refuse on public policy grounds. Promoting an unlicensed collection carries a fine up to RM5,000 or two years or both under s.3(2). Note also that the licensing machinery in s.2 is defined only for Peninsular Malaysia.
- REPLACE WITH: "If you are appealing to the public for money door to door, or soliciting in a street or other public place, that is a 'collection' under the House to House and Street Collections Act 1947 and it needs a licence from the Chief Police Officer for the area, or the Inspector-General across Peninsular Malaysia. Promoting an unlicensed collection carries a fine up to RM5,000 or two years' imprisonment or both. Selling tickets to a benefit gig is not a collection and neither is an online crowdfunder, but passing a bucket at a public event is exactly what the Act is aimed at. The machinery in the Act is Peninsular; check your state position in Sabah and Sarawak."

### [merch-online#08]: merch is goods, so service tax is the wrong tax to point a seller at
- WRONG: "Service tax reaches some supplies once you pass the threshold."
- WHY: Merchandise, prints and physical products are **goods**. The tax that reaches goods is sales tax under the Sales Tax Act 2018 (Act 806), charged on taxable goods manufactured in Malaysia by a registered manufacturer or on imported goods, not service tax under Act 807. An artist who buys finished shirts and resells them is not a manufacturer and is generally outside sales tax registration altogether; an artist who has goods made to order may need to look at the contract-manufacture and sub-contractor rules. Service tax only becomes relevant if the artist is supplying a **taxable service** within the First Schedule to the Service Tax Regulations, and the registration thresholds are category-specific (commonly RM500,000, but RM1,500,000 for food and beverage and RM1,000,000 for rental or leasing and financial services after the 2025 changes), and the rate is 6 or 8 per cent depending on the service. Pointing a merch seller at Act 807 and not at Act 806 is a mis-signpost, and Act 806 is not in the library at all.
- REPLACE WITH: "Selling physical merch is selling goods, so the tax to look at first is sales tax under the Sales Tax Act 2018, not service tax. Sales tax bites on goods manufactured here by a registered manufacturer and on imports; if you buy in finished stock and resell it you are usually outside it, and if you have goods made to order ask whether the contract-manufacture rules catch you. Service tax under the Service Tax Act 2018 only matters if you are supplying a taxable service, and the rate and the registration threshold both depend on which service it is, so check the current schedule against RMCD rather than a remembered figure."

## FLAGGED BUT DEFENSIBLE

- **[dj-club-night#14] Service Tax Act 2018.** "Once past the registration threshold, event and related services can be taxable. The list is widened by order most years, so check the current schedule rather than a remembered figure." This is the right way to write about service tax and the resource deserves credit for refusing to state a number it cannot stand behind. For the panel's record: the rate is 6 or 8 per cent depending on the service, and the registration thresholds are category-specific (RM500,000 generally, RM1,500,000 for food and beverage, and RM1,000,000 for rental or leasing and financial services following the 1 July 2025 expansion). Leave the claim as drafted.

- **[run-organisation#01] Partnership Act 1961, "joint and several liability".** The practical point is sound but the label is imprecise. Section 11 makes every partner liable **jointly** with the others for debts and obligations of the firm incurred while a partner, and makes his estate **severally** liable after death. Joint and several liability proper comes through ss.12 to 14, for loss or injury caused by a partner's wrongful act or omission and for misapplication of money. The practical exposure (each partner good for the whole debt) is right either way, so this is a precision fix rather than a correction; if it is edited, say "each of you is liable for the firm's debts, and for a partner's wrongdoing you are liable jointly and severally".

- **[exhibit-sell-art#08], [publish-book#09], [merch-online#10] badges of trade.** "Whether your sales are taxable business income turns on the badges of trade, not on whether you call yourself a business" is a fair statement. The badges are judicial, not statutory, so no section can be cited; the statutory hooks are s.3 (charge on income accruing in or derived from Malaysia), the s.2 definition of "business" as including a profession, vocation and trade, and s.4(a). Worth adding that even a receipt that fails the badges of trade can still be taxable under s.4(f) as a gain or profit not falling under the other paragraphs, so "not a business" is not the same as "not taxable".

- **[management-deal#06], [unpaid#09] Arbitration Act 2005.** Accurate. Section 10(1) requires the court, on the application of a party made **before taking any other step in the proceedings**, to stay and refer to arbitration unless the agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed. The claim's "a court must stay proceedings" is right; the timing condition is the practical trap and is worth adding, because a party who files a defence first loses the stay.

- **[merch-online#06] Electronic Commerce Act 2006.** "Electronic contracts and signatures are legally effective, so your checkout terms bind." Correct, and the hosted consolidation at 1 November 2012 is current: the Act has never been amended (the AGC reprint's list of amendments reads "Nil"). Two qualifications worth a clause: s.3 makes use of electronic messages consensual (though consent may be inferred from conduct), and the Schedule takes four things outside the Act entirely; powers of attorney, the creation of wills and codicils, the creation of trusts, and negotiable instruments. An artist doing estate or trust planning for a catalogue cannot do it by e-signature.

- **[run-organisation#02] Companies Act 2016 s.45.** Confirmed against the hosted text. Section 45(1) reserves the listed objects, including "promoting art", to a company limited by guarantee; s.45(2)(b) prohibits the payment of any dividend to members; s.45(3) allows application to the Minister for a licence to omit "Berhad" or "Bhd.". Add s.45(4), which the claim omits: a company limited by guarantee **shall not hold land** unless a licence has been obtained from the Minister. An arts CLBG that buys or is gifted a studio or gallery needs that licence and will not find out from this page.

- **[dj-club-night#07], [foreign-artist#03]; the withholding is a final tax.** Not stated, but correct and worth adding in one clause: for a non-resident public entertainer the 15 per cent is a final tax, so the artist does not file and cannot claim expenses against it. That is what makes the net-fee negotiation so brutal and it is the fact that most helps a promoter explain the deduction to an artist's agent.

- **[foreign-artist#02] Immigration Act 1959/63 and the Professional Visit Pass.** Correct in substance. The pass is issued by the Immigration Department as a Visit Pass (Professional), commonly Pas Lawatan Ikhtisas, and the sequence is PUSPAL approval first, then the pass. Two operational points to add: the applicant must be outside Malaysia when the application is made and enters on an entry visa, and Sabah and Sarawak run their own immigration control, so a pass for Peninsular Malaysia does not carry a performer into Kuching or Kota Kinabalu. The resource does flag the Sabah and Sarawak point at [foreign-artist#05]; it belongs next to the pass claim as well.

- **[stage-show#05], [dj-club-night#06], [foreign-artist#06] Entertainments Duty as a budget line.** The underlying warning (promoters price the ticket without it) is right and worth keeping. See the corrections above on the collector and on the statutory exemptions.

- **[run-organisation#03] Societies Act 1966 (Revised 2021), [run-organisation#04] LLP Act 2012, [archive#01] and [exhibit-sell-art#06] and [mural#03] National Heritage Act 2005, [archive#02] National Archives Act 2003, [archive#07] National Visual Arts Development Board Act 2011.** These are descriptive signposts rather than propositions of law and each is a fair summary of what its Act does. Nothing in them needs correcting in this scope. The one substantive addition worth making is at [run-organisation#03]: the Registrar's powers the claim refers to sit in ss.7, 13 and 14 of the Societies Act, and naming them turns a general warning into something a committee can actually look up.

## CONFIRMED

- **s.109A rate.** Schedule 1, Part II, paragraph 3: "Income of a non-resident person (other than a company) consisting of remuneration or other income in respect of services performed or rendered in Malaysia by a public entertainer … 15% of gross". The 15 per cent figure repeated across [dj-club-night#07], [foreign-artist#03] and the foreign-artist lede is correct.
- **s.109A mechanism.** "109A. The provisions of sections 109 and 110 shall apply mutatis mutandis to remuneration or other income in respect of services performed or rendered in Malaysia by a public entertainer." The resource's description of s.109A as importing the payer's machinery is correct.
- **Payer liability.** Section 109(1) puts the duty to deduct and to account within one month on the payer, not the artist. Correct as claimed.
- **The 10 per cent increase.** Section 109(2): "that amount which he fails to pay shall be increased by a sum equal to ten per cent of the amount which he fails to pay, and that amount and the increased sum shall be a debt due from him to the Government and shall be payable forthwith to the Director General." Correct as claimed.
- **"Public entertainer" catches a DJ.** The s.2 definition extends to "an individual who uses his intellectual, artistic, musical, personal or physical skill or character in" carrying out any activity in connection with any purpose through live or other medium. A DJ is inside it.
- **[sync-licence#04] Stamp Act.** "An unstamped licence is inadmissible until duty and penalty are paid" is the correct statement of s.52(1) and its proviso. This is the version the other Stamp Act claims should be aligned to.
- **[management-deal#05], [unpaid#07] Limitation Act.** Six years for contract and tort is s.6(1)(a). Section 29 postponing time for fraud, concealment by fraud or relief from a mistake is correct as drafted, including the "concealed by fraud" formulation.
- **[run-organisation#01] Partnership Act s.26 default split.** Section 26(a) gives partners an equal share in capital and profits and equal contribution to losses in the absence of agreement. "Section 26 arrives uninvited in most band disputes" is right.
- **[run-organisation#02] CLBG cannot pay dividends and needs a Minister's licence to drop "Berhad".** Both confirmed on the face of s.45(2)(b) and s.45(3).
- **[foreign-artist#01], [dj-club-night#09] PUSPAL exists and precedes the pass.** Confirmed. PUSPAL operates under the Ministry of Communications, applications run through the ePUSPAL system, and clearance is required before the Immigration Department will issue the Visit Pass (Professional). The claim that its conditions are administrative and change without gazettement is also right and is the correct thing to warn artists about.
- **[unpaid#01] Contracts Act s.74.** Section 74(1) gives compensation for loss or damage "which naturally arose in the usual course of things from the breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach of it", and s.74(2) excludes remote and indirect loss. The claim is accurate. The observation that a WhatsApp thread plus a delivered job is a contract is also right, subject only to stamping and evidential points covered elsewhere.
- **[unpaid#05], [exhibit-sell-art#04] Tribunal for Consumer Claims.** Correct that it is cheap, needs no lawyer and cuts both ways. Add the jurisdictional ceiling: the Tribunal cannot hear a claim where the total amount in respect of which an award is sought exceeds RM50,000.
- **[management-deal#06], [unpaid#09] Arbitration Act 2005 s.10 stay.** Confirmed.

- **The descriptive Contracts Act claims are reviewed and unobjectionable.** [sync-licence#03] (term, territory, media, exclusivity, renewal), [exhibit-sell-art#02] (consignment, commission percentage, insurance while held, gallery insolvency), [mural#06] (ownership, alteration, removal, who carries the council risk), [foreign-artist#07] (booking agreement, rider, cancellation, who carries the visa risk) and [someone-used-my-work#04] (breach of contract often the stronger claim) are checklists of what a contract should address rather than propositions of law. Each is a fair checklist and none needs correcting. The s.28 correction above applies only where a claim makes a legal assertion about restraint of trade.
- **[merch-online#09] Companies Act 2016, "the vehicle you sell through, and whether you chose one at all."** Descriptive and correct. The unincorporated-association point at material omission 6 is the useful addition here too.
- **[dj-club-night#08] Immigration Act 1959/63.** "A performance is work, so a foreign DJ needs the right pass. A social visit pass is the wrong pass and the exposure runs to the person who brought them in." Correct as stated; the operational additions at [foreign-artist#02] in the flagged section apply equally here.
- **[brand-deal#01] Consumer Protection Act 1999.** "Misleading conduct and false representation reach an endorsement. Saying you use something you do not is inside this Act, not just inside a platform policy." Correct: Part III of the Act covers misleading and deceptive conduct and false representations, and it binds the person who makes the representation, so a creator is exposed in their own right and not only through the brand. Worth adding that the Act is enforced by KPDN and that a breach is a criminal offence, not merely a civil wrong.
- **[brand-deal#10] Income Tax Act, "payment in kind is still income. Gifted product has a value."** Correct. Gifted product received for a campaign is a receipt of the creator's business or vocation and is brought in at its value, and the same logic applies to a comped trip or a gifted device. The practical addition is that the creator should record the market value at the time of receipt, because that is also the figure that goes into the s.44(6) or expense analysis later.
- **[run-organisation#10] Income Tax Act, "tax-exempt status is not automatic and is separate from the vehicle you chose."** Correct, and it is the right point to make right after the CLBG claim at [run-organisation#02]. Incorporating a company limited by guarantee confers no tax exemption at all. The exemption route is paragraph 13(1)(a) of Schedule 6, which exempts the income of an institution, organisation or fund **approved for the purposes of subsection 44(6)** so long as it complies with the conditions of that approval; s.44(6) is the separate application to the Director General that also makes a donor's gift deductible, capped at ten per cent of the donor's aggregate income. Name those two provisions and the claim becomes actionable.

## MATERIAL OMISSIONS

1. **Schedule 6 of the Income Tax Act exempts exactly this readership's income, and not one of the nine act-53 claims mentions it.** This is the largest omission in the tax scope and it costs readers money. Paragraph 32 exempts RM10,000 a year of a resident individual's royalty or payment for the publication, use or right to use any **artistic work** (other than an original painting) and royalty on recording discs or tapes. Paragraph 32B exempts RM20,000 of royalty (other than on discs or tapes) or payment for publication, use or right to use any **literary work or any original painting**. Paragraph 32D exempts RM20,000 of payment in respect of any **musical composition**. Paragraph 32A exempts RM12,000 for translation of books or literary work at the specific request of an agency of the Ministry of Education, the Ministry of Higher Education or the Attorney General's Chambers. Paragraph 32C exempts, without a cap, a resident individual's income in respect of performances in **cultural performances approved by the Minister**. Each of 32A, 32C and 32D is disapplied where the payment is part of the individual's emoluments in the exercise of official duties. [release-track#08], [publish-book#09], [exhibit-sell-art#08], [sync-licence#05], [brand-deal#10] and [merch-online#10] should each carry the relevant one.
2. **s.39(1)(q) disallowance.** Covered as a correction above. It is the difference between a 16.5 per cent problem and a 40 per cent one.
3. **Foreign-source income received in Malaysia.** A Malaysian artist paid by an overseas distributor, label, sync agent or platform is receiving foreign-source income in Malaysia. Paragraph 28 of Schedule 6 no longer exempts it generally, but the Income Tax (Exemption) (No. 5) Order 2022, P.U. (A) 234/2022, exempts a resident **individual** on all classes of foreign-source income received in Malaysia (other than from a partnership business in Malaysia) from 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2036, **on condition that the income has been subjected to tax of a similar character in the territory where it arises**. That condition is the trap: streaming and distribution income routed through a territory that imposed no tax on it may not qualify, and the individual must still declare it and keep the supporting evidence. None of the act-53 claims mentions this and it affects nearly every releasing artist.
4. **PDPA s.45(1) and s.45(2)(f) exemptions.** Seven PDPA claims tell artists they are inside the Act and none tells them about the two exemptions written for them. Section 45(1) exempts from the whole Act "personal data processed by an individual only for the purposes of that individual's personal, family or household affairs, including recreational purposes". Section 45(2)(f) exempts personal data "processed only for journalistic, literary or artistic purposes" from the General Principle, the Notice and Choice Principle, the Disclosure Principle, the Retention Principle, the Data Integrity Principle and the Access Principle, provided the processing is undertaken with a view to publication, the data user reasonably believes publication would be in the public interest taking into account the special importance of public interest in freedom of expression, and reasonably believes compliance is incompatible with the journalistic, literary or artistic purposes. Note what it does **not** exempt: the Security Principle survives, and so do the post-2024 breach notification and DPO duties. For a documentary maker at [documentary#04] this is the single most important provision in the Act and it is absent.
5. **Specific Relief Act: what you can and cannot get against a person.** [management-deal#04], [unpaid#08] and [someone-used-my-work#05] tell artists the Specific Relief Act 1950 gives them injunctions and rescission, without the rule that shapes every artist dispute. Section 20(1)(a) bars specific performance where compensation in money is adequate, and personal-service contracts are not specifically enforceable; s.54(f) bars an injunction to prevent the breach of a contract the performance of which would not be specifically enforced. But s.55 then provides that where a contract couples an affirmative agreement with a negative agreement, express or implied, the court's inability to compel the affirmative agreement does not preclude it granting an injunction to perform the negative one, provided the applicant has not himself failed to perform. That is precisely how a label or manager restrains an artist mid-term, and it is the mechanical partner to the s.28 point above: no court will order you to sing, but a court may well order you not to sing for anyone else.
6. **Registration of Businesses Act 1956 and the unincorporated association.** [run-organisation#01] says a band that signed nothing is a partnership by operation of law. Two things follow that the page does not say. First, a partnership carrying on business in Peninsular Malaysia must register with SSM within thirty days of commencement, and failure is an offence; that is the cheapest compliance step in the whole scenario and the Act is not in the library. Second, the s.3(1) test is "carrying on business in common **with a view of profit**". A genuinely non-profit collective therefore is **not** a partnership; it is an unincorporated association with no legal personality, where the committee contracts personally and there is no Partnership Act to fall back on. That is a worse position, not a better one, and the "choose the vehicle before you take the first grant" watch-claim is aimed at exactly this reader.
7. **Companies Act s.45(4): a CLBG cannot hold land without a Minister's licence.** Noted above.
8. **Public Authorities Protection Act 1948 and judicial review time limits.** [management-deal#05], [unpaid#07] and [someone-used-my-work#12] give six years without qualification. Where the counterparty is a public authority; a council, a state arts body, a public university gallery, a statutory agency; a much shorter limitation period applies under the Public Authorities Protection Act 1948, and a challenge to a licensing or permit decision by judicial review must be filed within three months of the decision under Order 53 of the Rules of Court 2012. I have not verified the PAPA section number against a hosted text (the Act is not in the library), so it should be cited only after that check, but the resource must not leave artists who contract with or are refused by government bodies believing they have six years.
9. **Withholding on royalties paid out of Malaysia.** [release-track#08] and [sync-licence#05] say withholding applies to royalties paid to non-residents and that the payer carries it, which is right. Neither gives the rate. Paragraph 2 of Part II of Schedule 1 charges a non-resident's royalty derived from Malaysia at **10 per cent of gross**, and the same s.109(2) ten per cent increase applies on failure. Give the number.
10. **Double taxation agreements.** Nothing in the resource mentions that a DTA may alter the position. It usually will not help a performer; the artistes and sportspersons article in most Malaysian treaties preserves the source country's right to tax; but some treaties carry an exemption for performances substantially supported by public funds or under a cultural exchange programme, which is directly relevant to festival and council-funded touring. One sentence pointing readers at the relevant treaty article would be a real service.

## PUBLICATION DEFECTS

1. **Two hosted PDFs have no text layer at all.** `legislation/pdf/act-135-bi.pdf` (Partnership Act 1961) and `legislation/pdf/act-91-bi.pdf` (Courts of Judicature Act 1964) return nothing at all through `pdftotext`. They are image scans. They cannot be searched, cannot be copied from, cannot be read by a screen reader and cannot be quoted. Act 135 is the sole authority behind [run-organisation#01], a claim about liability that the reader is being told to act on. Publishing a liability claim against an unreadable scan is not acceptable for a law NGO. Replace both with the AGC text-layer PDFs before launch.
2. **The Limitation Act 1953 is hosted in Bahasa Malaysia only, and the wrong vintage.** There is no `act-254-bi.pdf`; only `act-254-bm.pdf`. That file's own cover states "Sebagaimana pada 1 Mei 2013" (as at 1 May 2013), while the claims metadata presents the instrument as consolidated to 1 September 2019. Worse, the AGC translation carries the caveat on its own face that the text is **only a translation** and is not authoritative unless prescribed as authentic. Three claims about limitation periods ([management-deal#05], [unpaid#07], [someone-used-my-work#12]) rest on it. Source the English reprint and correct the "as at" date shown on the page.
3. **The Personal Data Protection Act is hosted as at 1 July 2023 and the claims assert 2024 law.** [post-set-online#07], [documentary#04] and [merch-online#07] tell readers about breach notification and the DPO duty. Neither is in the hosted text, because the Personal Data Protection (Amendment) Act 2024 commenced in phases on 1 January, 1 April and 1 June 2025. A reader who clicks through to check will not find the duty they were told about, and will reasonably conclude either that the resource is wrong or that the duty does not exist. Either host the amended consolidation or put a dated banner on the PDF link saying in terms that the hosted text predates the 2024 amendments and naming what they added.
4. **Eleven in-scope instruments are presented as current law on 2006-era consolidations.** Act 136 (Contracts Act, "up to 1 January 2006"), Act 137 (Specific Relief Act, 1 January 2006), Act 155 (Immigration Act, 1 January 2006), Act 300 (Distribution Act, 1 January 2006), Act 629 (National Archives Act, 1 January 2006), Act 645 (National Heritage Act, 1 June 2006), Act 653 (ASWARA Act, 31 August 2006) and Act 135 (Partnership Act, shown in the claims metadata as 1 June 2006; the PDF itself cannot be read to confirm it) are all twenty-year-old reprints. For most of them that is genuinely fine, because they have not been amended, but the page cannot expect the reader to know which. Every instrument page should carry a one-line amendment status: "no amendments since this reprint, verified [date]" or "amended by Act ___, not reflected in this text".
5. **A counter-example worth copying.** The Electronic Commerce Act 2006 reprint carries its own "list of amendments; Nil" table. That is exactly the assurance every other instrument in the library needs, and the site should surface it rather than leaving it on page 17 of a PDF.
6. **Three in-scope instruments are hosted but carry no claim at all.** Act 91 (Courts of Judicature Act 1964), Act 67 (Civil Law Act 1956) and Act 653 (ASWARA Act 2006) appear in `legislation/pdf/` and in no scenario in `by-instrument.txt`. For Act 67 that is a real gap rather than a tidy-up: s.3 of the Civil Law Act is the reception provision that lets Malaysian courts apply English common law and equity, and it is the foundation of passing off, which the resource relies on at [release-track#03] and [someone-used-my-work#02] without ever saying where it comes from.
7. **The Sales Tax Act 2018 (Act 806) is not in the library**, although [merch-online#08] sends a goods seller to the Service Tax Act. See the correction above.
8. **The non-Act entries carry a PDF path that does not resolve.** `cmos`, `puspal`, `berne`, `wppt` and `marrakesh` all display `legislation/pdf/<id>-bi.pdf` and no such file exists. If the page renders that path as a link, five entries ship with a dead download. They should render as a described-and-linked source with an outbound link to the Ministry of Communications ePUSPAL portal, to WIPO and to the societies' own tariff pages.
