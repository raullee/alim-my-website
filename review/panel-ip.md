# PANEL REVIEW — INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
Scope: act-332, act-815, act-552, act-291, act-836, act-606, act-730, cmos, berne, wppt, marrakesh (38 claims plus the scenario ledes and watch-claims that carry IP propositions).
Primary text used: the hosted AGC consolidations in `legislation/pdf/`, checked for post-consolidation amendment.

## VERDICT

PUBLISH AFTER CORRECTIONS — the resource cites a deleted subsection in three places, names a de-registered collecting society in five places, omits the body that actually licenses sound recordings, and points artists at a repealed enforcement route. Every error is identified and drop-in text is supplied below.

## CORRECTIONS REQUIRED

### [dj-club-night — WATCH-CLAIM]
- WRONG: "MACP, PRISM and RPM are three separate rights held by three separate bodies."
- WHY: PRISM Berhad's declaration as a licensing body was **revoked by MyIPO** and it no longer licenses in Malaysia. RPM is **Recording Performers Malaysia Berhad** and represents recording *performers*, not the recording. The body that licenses the *sound recording* on behalf of record companies is **PPM (Public Performance Malaysia Berhad)**, which the resource never mentions. MyIPO's current declared CMO list for musical works and sound recordings is MACP, PPM, RPM and Music Right (Sabah) Berhad; PRISM and MRM are both listed as revoked. The joint body MRM was dissolved and its status revoked, and MACP, PPM and RPM resumed licensing separately, each setting its own tariff. Source: MyIPO, *Collective Management Organization* register; joint announcement of MACP, PPM and RPM on the dissolution of MRM.
- REPLACE WITH: The single most common failure is the room assuming one licence covers the music. It does not. MACP, PPM and RPM are three separate bodies collecting on three separate rights, and each sets its own tariff.

### [dj-club-night#01]
- WRONG: "MACP for the composition, PRISM for the performers, RPM for the recording."
- WHY: As above. The roles of PRISM and RPM are swapped and PRISM is defunct; PPM is missing. The statutory basis also differs between them: MACP and PPM licence exclusive rights under s.13(1)(aa) and (b) Copyright Act 1987 ("Copyright in a literary, musical or artistic work, a film, or a sound recording ... shall be the exclusive right to control in Malaysia ... (aa) the communication to the public; (b) the performance, showing or playing to the public"), whereas the performers' claim RPM administers is a right to payment under s.16B ("an equitable remuneration for the performance shall be payable to the performer by the user of the sound recording"), not a right to stop the play.
- REPLACE WITH: Playing recorded music to a paying room engages three separate claims held by three separate bodies: MACP for the musical work, PPM for the sound recording, and RPM for the performers on it. A room holding one of the three is under-licensed, and the liability lands on whoever put the night on.

### [release-track#02]
- WRONG: "Register with MACP as a writer and PRISM as a performer or you collect nothing from public performance and broadcast."
- WHY: PRISM no longer exists as a declared body. Performer royalties from public performance and broadcast are administered by RPM (Recording Performers Malaysia Berhad). A recording artist who is also a label or rights owner of the master deals with PPM separately.
- REPLACE WITH: Register with MACP as a writer and RPM as a performer, or you collect nothing from public performance and broadcast no matter how heavily you are played. If you own the master, PPM is a third registration.

### [sync-licence#02]
- WRONG: "A blanket public performance licence from MACP, PRISM or RPM is not a sync licence."
- WHY: Body naming only; the substance is right. A blanket licence issued under a licensing scheme covers the acts in s.27AA(1) for which the scheme is operated, and synchronisation with a film is not one of them; the acts a sync requires are reproduction and adaptation of the musical work and reproduction of the sound recording, cleared directly.
- REPLACE WITH: A blanket public performance licence from MACP, PPM or RPM is not a sync licence and never has been. Sync is negotiated directly with the owners.

### [stage-show#02]
- WRONG: "the public performance rights administered by MACP, PRISM and RPM."
- WHY: As above.
- REPLACE WITH: Live and recorded music in the show engages the public performance rights administered by MACP and PPM, and the performers' remuneration collected by RPM.

### [someone-used-my-work#06]
- WRONG: "the route is the licensing bodies and their tariffs, not a lawsuit."
- WHY: The body naming is fine here, but the sentence tells an owner that litigation is not available, which is wrong. Collective licensing is how *users* get permission; an owner whose work is infringed retains the s.37 action, and ss.41 and 43 criminal liability sits alongside it. The Tribunal's jurisdiction under ss.27B to 27L is over licensing schemes and licence terms, which is a dispute a *licensee or prospective licensee* brings, not a substitute for an owner's infringement claim.
- REPLACE WITH: If the use is music being performed or broadcast, the practical route is usually the licensing body and its tariff rather than a claim of your own. A dispute about a licensing scheme or the terms of a licence goes to the Copyright Tribunal; your own infringement action under section 37 is not displaced by it.

### [exhibit-sell-art#03]
- WRONG: "s.7(6) of the Copyright Act cuts your copyright and registration under this Act becomes the protection."
- WHY: **Section 7(6) has been deleted.** The hosted text reads "(6) (Deleted by Act A1420)." Two provisions now do this work. Section 7(5): "Copyright shall not subsist under this Act in any design which is registered under any written law relating to industrial design." Section 13B(2), which applies where an artistic work has been exploited by the copyright owner or under licence by making articles by an industrial process and marketing them: "After the end of the period of twenty-five years from the end of the calendar year in which such articles are first marketed, the work may be copied by making articles of any description ... without infringing copyright in the work." So copyright is not cut on industrial application; it is cut back to twenty-five years, and only where the owner has itself industrially exploited and marketed.
- REPLACE WITH: If you industrially produce and market articles made to your own artistic work, section 13B limits copyright in that work to twenty-five years from the end of the year the articles are first marketed. And if the design is registered under industrial design law, section 7(5) removes copyright in it altogether. Registration under this Act is the protection designers are meant to be relying on, and they fall into this gap constantly.

### [someone-used-my-work#03]
- WRONG: "s.7(6) of the Copyright Act may already have cut the copyright you were relying on."
- WHY: As above. Section 7(6) is deleted.
- REPLACE WITH: If it is the shape or ornament of a mass-produced article, registration under this Act is the protection. Check section 13B of the Copyright Act first: twenty-five years after articles made to the work were first marketed, the copyright you were relying on no longer stops copying.

### [merch-online#03]
- WRONG: "The design of a mass-produced article is protected by registration, not by copyright, and novelty is destroyed by showing it first."
- WHY: Two problems. First, copyright is not displaced by mass production; section 13B leaves twenty-five years of copyright running from first marketing, and section 7(5) only removes copyright where the design is actually registered. Second, the novelty point is right but the hosted PDF is a 2013 consolidation that predates the Industrial Designs (Amendment) Act 2013 [Act A1449]. A1449 s.3 amended s.12(2)(a) "by inserting after the word 'Malaysia' the words 'or elsewhere'", so novelty is now destroyed by disclosure **anywhere in the world**, not only in Malaysia. The only grace is s.12(3): six months before filing, and only for an official or officially recognised exhibition, or a disclosure by someone else as a result of an unlawful act. There is no general grace period for the designer's own posting, showing or selling.
- REPLACE WITH: The design of a mass-produced article is protected by registration. Copyright is not a substitute: section 13B of the Copyright Act runs it out twenty-five years after the articles are first marketed. Novelty is destroyed by disclosure anywhere in the world before you file, and there is no grace period for showing your own work; the only six-month exception is for an official exhibition or a disclosure someone else made unlawfully.

### [merch-online#04]
- WRONG: "False trade description is the enforcement route against counterfeit merchandise, in both directions."
- WHY: The trade mark limb of the Trade Descriptions Act 2011 is gone. Sections 8 and 9, which carried the registered trade mark provisions and the trade description order procedure, both read "(Deleted by Act A1607)" in the hosted consolidation. Those criminal and enforcement provisions were consolidated into the Trademarks Act 2019 Part XV: s.99 (counterfeiting a registered trademark, fine to RM1,000,000 or five years or both), s.100 (falsely applying a registered trademark) and s.102 (dealing in goods with a falsely applied mark). The Trade Descriptions Act still bites on false descriptions of goods generally under s.5, read with ss.6, 7 and 10, but it is no longer the route for a counterfeit mark.
- REPLACE WITH: Counterfeit merchandise carrying a registered mark is prosecuted under Part XV of the Trademarks Act 2019, not under the trade description order route, which was repealed. The Trade Descriptions Act still reaches false claims about the goods themselves, such as origin, maker or composition, and that cuts in both directions.

### [someone-used-my-work#09]
- WRONG: "Where the copy is being sold as goods, false trade description is the enforcement route and it carries criminal penalties."
- WHY: As above. The registered-mark route is now the Trademarks Act 2019, Part XV. Naming the Trade Descriptions Act as the route sends an owner to a repealed procedure.
- REPLACE WITH: Where the copy is being sold as goods under your registered mark, the criminal route is Part XV of the Trademarks Act 2019, enforced by the Ministry of Domestic Trade. If the falsity is about the goods rather than the mark, the Trade Descriptions Act 2011 applies instead. Both carry criminal penalties.

### [release-track#09]
- WRONG: "Still the live instrument for a physical pressing run."
- WHY: The Act is in force, but it does not regulate pressing runs in general and it does not regulate the label. Section 4 makes it an offence for a person to *manufacture optical discs* without a licence, and s.3(2) defines being engaged in manufacture as owning, directing, managing or controlling the business that undertakes it. "Optical disc" is defined in s.2 as a medium or device in Schedule 1 or one "on which data may be stored in digital form and read by means of a laser". Vinyl and cassette are outside the Act entirely. The label's exposure is in choosing a plant, not in holding a licence.
- REPLACE WITH: This licenses the plant, not you. Anyone manufacturing optical discs without a licence commits an offence, so check your replicator holds a current one before you place a CD or DVD order. It does not touch vinyl or cassette, which are not optical media.

### [release-track#01]
- WRONG: "s.27 requires assignments and exclusive licences to be in writing. Malaysia has no copyright registration: protection is automatic on fixation."
- WHY: Two errors. (1) Section 27(3) is wider than exclusive licences: "No assignment of copyright and no licence to do an act the doing of which is controlled by copyright shall have effect unless it is in writing." Every licence is caught, exclusive or not. Telling artists only exclusive licences need writing invites them to treat an oral or chat-message non-exclusive permission as effective, and it is not. (2) Malaysia does have a register. Section 26A provides for Voluntary Notification to the Controller, s.26B establishes the Register of Copyright, and s.26B(5) makes certified extracts "prima facie evidence of the particulars entered therein ... admissible in all courts". It is not constitutive of the right but it is not nothing.
- REPLACE WITH: Two rights live in every release: the composition and the sound recording, often owned by different people. Section 26 gives first ownership and s.26(2) quietly moves it for commissioned work and work made in employment. Section 27(3) is stricter than most people assume: no assignment and no licence of any kind, exclusive or not, has effect unless it is in writing. Copyright itself arises automatically on fixation with no registration, but Voluntary Notification under s.26A puts your claim on a public register and a certified extract is prima facie evidence in court.

### [sync-licence#01]
- WRONG: "Section 27 requires an exclusive licence to be in writing."
- WHY: Section 27(3) requires *every* licence to be in writing, not only an exclusive one. In a sync scenario this is the point that matters: a non-exclusive sync licence agreed by email thread and never documented has no effect.
- REPLACE WITH: Section 27(3) requires every licence to be in writing, exclusive or not, so a sync agreed in conversation has no effect at all.

### [publish-book#02]
- WRONG: "Section 27 requires an exclusive licence to be in writing, and a publishing deal is usually an exclusive licence."
- WHY: Understates s.27(3), which catches all licences and all assignments. An author who reads this may think a non-exclusive territorial or serial licence can be left informal.
- REPLACE WITH: Section 27(3) requires every assignment and every licence to be in writing, exclusive or not, and a publishing deal is usually an exclusive licence. Anything you let a publisher do that is not in the written grant is not licensed.

### [management-deal#02]
- WRONG: "Section 27 requires assignments and exclusive licences to be in writing."
- WHY: As above; s.27(3) is not limited to exclusive licences.
- REPLACE WITH: Section 27(3) requires every assignment and every licence to be in writing, exclusive or not. Understand exactly what is assigned, for how long, and whether it reverts.

### [someone-used-my-work#01]
- WRONG: "There is no registration to fall back on; your evidence is files, dates and drafts."
- WHY: Directly contradicted by ss.26A to 26C. There is a Register of Copyright maintained by the Controller, and under s.26B(5) a certified extract is prima facie evidence of the particulars entered and is admissible in all courts. Section 42 also allows an affidavit of ownership to be admitted. An owner who was told there is "no registration to fall back on" will not have filed and will not ask for the extract.
- REPLACE WITH: Copyright arises without registration, but there is a register: Voluntary Notification under s.26A puts your claim on the Register of Copyright, and a certified extract is prima facie evidence of ownership admissible in court. If you never notified, your evidence is files, dates and drafts, and a statutory declaration of ownership under section 42.

### [archive#03]
- WRONG: "Copyright outlives the author by fifty years, so most archive material is still in copyright."
- WHY: True only for literary, musical and artistic works under s.17(1). Archive holdings are mostly the categories where the term is not measured from the author's death at all: sound recordings run fifty years from the year after first publication or, if unpublished, fixation (s.19); broadcasts fifty years from the year after the broadcast (s.20); films fifty years from the year after first publication (s.22). Unpublished literary, musical and artistic works have their own rule in s.17(2). Applying life-plus-fifty to a tape or a reel will produce the wrong answer in both directions.
- REPLACE WITH: Term depends on what the item is. Literary, musical and artistic works run for the author's life plus fifty years. Sound recordings, films and broadcasts run fifty years from the year after publication, fixation or broadcast, with no reference to anyone's death. Most archive material is still in copyright and often has no traceable owner, so duration and first ownership are the two questions, and you must ask them per item.

## FLAGGED BUT DEFENSIBLE

### [dj-club-night#02]
- CLAIM: "Your own edits, bootlegs and mashups are derivative works: making one is a permission, playing it is a second, releasing it is a third."
- RISK: Correct as far as it goes (s.8(1)(a); s.8(2) "without prejudice to any protection of the existing works used"; s.13(1)(a), (aa), (b)). It omits that the 2022 amendment inserted s.13(2)(b), which puts "parody, pastiche or caricature" outside the owner's control, and s.13(2A), which sets the fair dealing factors. There is no Malaysian authority on it yet, so I would not tell a DJ a mashup is safe; but the flat "three permissions" line now overstates the position and will read as out of date to any IP practitioner.

### [mural#02]
- CLAIM: "Section 25 moral rights, including integrity, are where the argument lands when a wall is painted over or altered."
- RISK: The right is real (s.25(2)(b), s.25(4), enforceable as a breach of statutory duty under s.25(5)), but the threshold is high and cumulative: the distortion, mutilation or modification must "significantly alter the work" **and** be such that it "might reasonably be regarded as adversely affecting the author's honour or reputation". Section 25(3) also lets an authorised party make modifications reasonably necessary to the authorised use. Outright destruction or painting over is arguably not a "modification" of the work at all, which is where the argument actually falls over in practice. Say the argument lands there; do not imply it succeeds.

### [release-track#03]
- CLAIM: "Registration gives a monopoly in the registered classes."
- RISK: Section 48(1) gives exclusive rights "in relation to the goods or services for which the trademark is registered". The specification, not the class, defines the scope; a class-wide monopoly is not what registration gives. The claim also understates the position for well-known marks, which s.54 protects against use on dissimilar goods in defined circumstances. Passing off remains available and is expressly preserved as an earlier right by s.24(4)(a).

### [release-track#04] and [publish-book#08]
- CLAIM: "You are protected in every member state automatically, with no registration or formality. That is the answer to whether you need to register your copyright overseas."
- RISK: Sound for literary, musical and artistic works. Malaysia has been a Berne party since 1 October 1990. Two gaps. Berne covers literary and artistic works; it does not cover sound recordings, so on a release page the master is not covered by the sentence at all, and the relevant instrument for that is WPPT. Second, in the United States, registration with the Copyright Office remains a precondition to statutory damages and attorneys' fees, which is the practical answer to "do I need to register overseas" for an artist licensing into the US. "No formality" is true of the existence of the right, not of remedies.

### [sync-licence#07]
- CLAIM: "National treatment means a Malaysian work is protected in the buyer's territory without formality."
- RISK: Conflates two distinct Berne principles. National treatment (Art. 5(1)) means a foreign work gets what local works get. The absence of formality is a separate rule (Art. 5(2)). They usually deliver the same practical answer, but the sentence as written will be read as wrong by anyone who knows the convention.

### [release-track#05]
- CLAIM: WPPT is "the source of the performers' rights provisions in the Copyright Act."
- RISK: Malaysia acceded to WPPT on 27 September 2012, in force 27 December 2012, and the Copyright (Amendment) Act 2012 [Act A1420] inserted the modern performers' rights architecture in the same period. Presenting causation rather than correlation is a stronger claim than the record supports. Malaysia also has performers' provisions predating that. Say WPPT is the international floor Malaysia's performers' rights are aligned to.

### [publish-book#07] and [archive#05]
- CLAIM: Marrakesh is "the basis for the accessible-format exceptions" / "the basis for making and sharing accessible-format copies without the rightholder's permission."
- RISK: Malaysia acceded on 31 March 2022, in force 30 June 2022, and the Copyright (Amendment) Act 2022 brought the exceptions in on the same day. But Malaysia is dualist: the operative law is the Copyright Act, not the treaty, and a librarian or publisher relying on "the Marrakesh Treaty" is relying on the wrong instrument. The domestic provisions are s.13(2)(ggggg) (making and issuing accessible format copies), s.13(2)(r) and (s) (import, and export to a Marrakesh member), and s.25(3A) (no moral rights breach). The catch that matters: under s.13(2C) read with the s.3 definition, an "authorized entity" is one **prescribed by the Minister**. An archive or library that has not been prescribed cannot rely on the entity limb and must work through the print-disabled person or someone acting on their behalf. I did not verify whether any prescribing order has been made; the resource should not assume one has.

### [post-set-online#02]
- CLAIM: "Platform-level licensing covers some uses and not others, and never covers a full recorded set."
- RISK: Accurate as a description of platform practice, not a statement of Malaysian law, and "never" is an absolute about private contracts that change. Defensible if presented as commercial reality rather than a legal rule.

## MATERIAL OMISSIONS

1. **PPM does not appear anywhere in the resource.** The body that licenses the sound recording for public performance and communication to the public is absent from a resource whose most-repeated warning is about under-licensing. A venue that follows this page will still be unlicensed.
2. **MARC.** Malaysia Reprographic Rights Centre Berhad is a declared CMO for literary works. Nothing in publish-book or archive tells a publisher, library or archive it exists.
3. **Operating as a CMO without a declaration is an offence.** Section 27A(8) carries a fine up to RM500,000, and under s.27A(3) a declaration runs for two years and must be renewed. The practical use to an artist or venue is the reverse of what the resource covers: before paying a demand, check the body currently holds a declaration. PRISM and MRM both lost theirs while still being invoiced in the market.
4. **Equitable remuneration is the performer's own statutory claim.** Section 16B(1) makes it payable "to the performer by the user of the sound recording", and s.16B(3) preserves better contractual terms. Session and featured performers routinely sign masters away believing they have signed this away too. Section 16B(3A) also lets a performer go to the Tribunal where there is no contract on the amount.
5. **Freedom of panorama.** Section 13(2)(c) puts the inclusion in a film or broadcast of any artistic work situated where it can be viewed by the public outside the owner's control. This belongs in both mural and documentary, and it is the answer to a filmmaker's most common street-shooting question.
6. **Parody, pastiche and caricature.** Section 13(2)(b), new in 2022, is the single most useful provision to an artist in the whole Act and appears nowhere.
7. **Criminal copyright liability.** Sections 41 and 43 (offences and penalties), s.43A (anti-camcording) and s.43AA (streaming technology) create personal criminal exposure for exactly the conduct the club, post-set and release scenarios describe. The resource treats copyright as civil throughout.
8. **Technological protection measures.** Section 36A makes circumvention actionable, which is the real constraint on a DJ or archivist ripping protected files, and s.36B covers rights management information.
9. **No claims at all on the Patents Act 1983 or the Geographical Indications Act 2022**, although both PDFs are hosted. The GI Act is the live instrument for craft and produce identity such as textile, ceramic and food-origin names and is directly relevant to an arts NGO's constituency; act-836 repeals the Geographical Indications Act 2000 (s.100(1)). If the resource is not going to cover them, the hosted PDFs should not suggest it does.

**Publication defects to fix before launch.** Nine claims cite PDF paths that do not exist: `cmos-bi.pdf`, `berne-bi.pdf`, `wppt-bi.pdf`, `marrakesh-bi.pdf`, `puspal-bi.pdf`, `state-entertainment-bi.pdf`, `cmcf-content-code-bi.pdf`, `fedcon-bi.pdf`, `syariah-criminal-bi.pdf`. Every claim in my scope that turns on collecting societies or treaties links to a missing file. Separately, `act-552-bi.pdf` is the consolidation "As at 1 January 2013" showing Act A1140 (2003) as the latest amendment; it predates Act A1449, which took effect 1 July 2013 and changed both novelty (worldwide) and term (s.25(2), "two" further terms to "four", so five years extendable to twenty-five). Publishing that file as the current law of registered designs is itself a misstatement. `act-332-bi.pdf` is current: MyIPO's September 2025 upload is the same "As at 30 June 2022" text and A1645 remains the latest amendment.
