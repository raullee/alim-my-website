# Panel review: media law, constitutional law, criminal defence

Reviewer: senior counsel, media / constitutional / criminal defence.
Scope: fedcon; act-588; act-866; act-620; act-244; act-301; act-15; act-286; act-574; act-336; act-736; act-88; act-563; cmcf-content-code; syariah-criminal; state-entertainment; act-493; act-171.
Date of review: 21 September 2026. Verified against the hosted AGC consolidations in `legislation/pdf/`, checked forward for post-consolidation amendments.

## VERDICT

PUBLISH AFTER CORRECTIONS

Nothing here requires a scenario to be rewritten, but four of the findings below are not stylistic and must be fixed before this goes out under a law NGO's name. `post-set-online#06` tells an artist that a film they posted online needs Board approval, when s.2(3) of the Film Censorship Act 2002 says in terms that the Act is not to be construed as permitting censorship of anything transmitted over the internet. `busking#05` gives the wrong year and the wrong court for a case decided by the Federal Court in 2025. `publish-book#01` mis-describes the most consequential positive claim in the area and tells book authors they need a permit they do not need. `post-set-online#03` names an amending Act that does not exist, dates it to the wrong year, and omits both the statutory protection for satire and parody and the February 2026 Federal Court judgment reading s.233 down. Separately, `act-336-bi.pdf` is the Malay text, scanned, with no text layer, carrying an express notice that it is not legislation.

## CORRECTIONS REQUIRED

### [busking#05]

- WRONG: "the notification requirement in s.9(1) survived the 2021 challenge even though s.9(5) did not"
- WHY: There was no successful 2021 challenge. Section 9(5) of the Peaceful Assembly Act 2012 was declared unconstitutional by the **Federal Court on 1 July 2025** in *Amir Hariri bin Abd Hadi v Public Prosecutor*, as a disproportionate restriction exceeding what Article 10(2)(b) permits in relation to Article 10(1)(b). The earlier history runs the other way and must not be blurred into it: the Court of Appeal held s.9(5) unconstitutional in *Nik Nazmi bin Nik Ahmad v Public Prosecutor* (Court of Appeal, 2014), and that decision was then departed from in *Public Prosecutor v Yuneswaran a/l Ramaraj* (Court of Appeal, 2015), which is why s.9(5) remained enforceable until 2025. I have given those two by case name, court and year only; their report citations should be checked against the law reports before anyone prints them. Section 9(1) itself was not struck down; the five-day notification duty stands, but there is now no penalty attached to breaching it. The hosted PDF, as at 1 December 2019, still prints s.9(5) as live law, so a reader checking the PDF against the page will be misled unless the page says so.
- REPLACE WITH: "A crowd that forms around you can be characterised as an assembly. Section 9(1) still asks an organiser to notify the police five days ahead, but the offence of not doing so, s.9(5), was struck down as unconstitutional by the Federal Court on 1 July 2025 in Amir Hariri bin Abd Hadi v Public Prosecutor. The duty survives; the criminal penalty for breaching it does not. The consolidated text we host predates that ruling and still prints the offence."

### [publish-book#01]

Two separate errors in one claim, and this is the claim the scenario's watch-line is built on.

- WRONG (1): "publications need a permit"
- WHY: Under the Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984 the permit under ss.5 and 6 is required only for a **newspaper**, as defined in s.2. That definition is wide, and it does capture "any magazine, comic or other forms of periodical printed in any language for sale or free distribution at regular or irregular intervals"; so a zine issued in numbered instalments and a comic series are caught. But a one-off art book, an exhibition catalogue or a single monograph is not a newspaper and needs no permit. As drafted the claim tells an author of a book that they need a Home Ministry permit. They do not. What every publication is exposed to is s.7 prohibition, which is a different thing and should not be collapsed into the permit point.
- REPLACE WITH (1): "Printing presses need a licence under s.3. A permit under ss.5 and 6 is needed only for a 'newspaper', but s.2 defines that widely enough to take in magazines, comics and any periodical issued at regular or irregular intervals for sale or free distribution, so a numbered zine or a comic series is inside it. A one-off book or exhibition catalogue is not. Everything printed, whether or not it needs a permit, is still exposed to the Minister's prohibition power in s.7."

- WRONG (2): "The 2012 amendment removed the finality clause, so the Minister's decision is now judicially reviewable, and that is the opening."
- WHY: The finality clause was not removed. Section 13A, headed "Minister's decision final", is still on the statute book and still reads, in the text we host as at 1 September 2012, "Any decision of the Minister to refuse to grant or to revoke or to suspend a licence or permit shall be final." What the Printing Presses and Publications (Amendment) Act 2012 (Act A1436, in force 15 July 2012) removed were the **ouster words** that followed, namely the words excluding the decision from being called in question by any court on any ground whatsoever, together with the Minister's "absolute discretion" in ss.3 and 6 and the twelve-month expiry that forced annual renewal. That is what opened the door. Two further precisions matter: s.13A on its face only ever reached licence and permit decisions, not s.7 prohibition orders, and s.7(1) still confers the prohibition power on the Minister "in his absolute discretion", unamended. Telling an author the finality clause is gone, when the marginal note in the PDF they can download still says "Minister's decision final", will destroy confidence in the whole resource.
- REPLACE WITH (2): "The 2012 amendment did not delete s.13A, which is still headed 'Minister's decision final'. What it removed were the words that had barred any court from questioning the decision at all, along with the Minister's 'absolute discretion' over licences and permits and the twelve-month expiry that forced annual renewal. A refusal, revocation or suspension can now be taken to court on ordinary administrative law grounds. Note the limits: s.13A only ever covered licence and permit decisions, and the prohibition power in s.7(1) is still expressed to be exercisable in the Minister's absolute discretion."

### [post-set-online#03]

The whole claim needs rebuilding. It is wrong on the name of the amending Act, wrong on the year, incomplete on what s.233 now says, and silent on the two developments that matter most to an artist.

- WRONG (1): "Penalties were raised in 2024."
- WHY: There is no Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2024. The amending statute is **Act A1743, the Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2025**: Royal Assent 27 January 2025, gazetted 7 February 2025, and s.91 (the s.233 amendment) in force **11 February 2025**. Dating it to 2024 gets the only thing the date is for wrong, which is telling a reader which version applies to a post. Anything posted before 11 February 2025 is judged on the old penalty of a fine up to RM50,000 or one year or both, which is the figure still printed in the PDF we host.

- WRONG (2): "offensive, indecent, obscene or menacing content, and content sent with intent to annoy"
- WHY: Three problems. First, "false" is omitted, and for artists it is the most dangerous word in the subsection; satire and fiction are exactly what an investigating officer reaches for it over. Second, "offensive" was replaced by "**grossly offensive**" by s.91(a)(i) of Act A1743, which raises the threshold; an artist is entitled to know the bar went up, because it is the one amendment in the package that helps them. Third, s.91(a)(ii) widened the intent limb from "or harass another person" to ", harass or commit an offence involving fraud or dishonesty against, any person". The provision now reads, so far as relevant, "obscene, indecent, false, menacing or grossly offensive in character with intent to annoy, abuse, threaten, harass or commit an offence involving fraud or dishonesty against, any person". Note also that the penalty is now in s.233(2), not s.233(3) as the hosted text has it; s.233(3) is now the child-victim provision, a fine up to RM500,000 or five years.

- WRONG (3): omission. Act A1743 inserted six statutory Explanations into s.233 and they are the most artist-favourable text in Malaysian online speech law. Explanation 4: "Content in the form of satire and parody or where it is clear that it is fictional, are not false." Explanation 3: "Content that portrays private parts based on arts, information or science which are not gross such as sex education or anatomy, are not obscene or indecent." Explanation 6(d): "any communication made in good faith is not grossly offensive as long as the communication consists of statements of fact, that are true in substances and facts, and consists of statements of opinion." A resource for artists that reports the higher penalties and not the statutory carve-out for satire, parody, fiction and artistic nudity is reporting only the half that hurts.

- WRONG (4): omission. *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li*, Federal Court Civil Appeal No. 08(f)-35-02/2024(W), decided **6 February 2026**. The Court of Appeal had struck the words "offensive" and "annoy" out of s.233(1)(a) on 19 August 2025. The Federal Court, Nallini Pathmanathan FCJ, allowed the Government's appeal in part and reinstated both words, but held at the same time that Heidy Quah should never have been prosecuted because her post was fact and opinion and fell squarely inside Article 10(1)(a). The operative reasoning is directly usable by any artist: Article 10(1)(a) is "inherently qualified" so as to exclude communications intended to cause harm or injury, and s.233(1)(a) reaches only that excluded category; but "discontent or anger arising from the expression of political views and more controversial issues do not constitute harm", and such communications are protected speech. Any artist charged under s.233 after 6 February 2026 is being charged under a provision the Federal Court has read down, and the page must say so.
- REPLACE WITH: "Section 233 is the provision most often used against people who post. It reaches content that is obscene, indecent, false, menacing or grossly offensive in character, sent with intent to annoy, abuse, threaten, harass, or commit an offence involving fraud or dishonesty against, any person. The Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2025 (Act A1743) took effect on 11 February 2025; it raised the maximum to a fine of RM500,000 or two years or both, but it also raised the threshold from 'offensive' to 'grossly offensive' and wrote in Explanations that say satire, parody and clearly fictional work are not 'false', that artistic or anatomical depiction of the body is not obscene or indecent, and that a good-faith statement of fact or opinion is not grossly offensive. In February 2026 the Federal Court in Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li kept the words 'offensive' and 'annoy' in the section but read it down: it reaches communications intended to cause harm, and anger or discontent provoked by political or controversial expression is protected speech, not harm. The consolidated text we host predates all of this. This is the MCMC route."

### [post-set-online#06]

- WRONG: "If what you posted is a film, Board approval is the gate, and the Board reads film widely enough to catch shorts, music videos and festival material."
- WHY: Flatly contrary to the Act. Section 2(3) of the Film Censorship Act 2002 says, in terms: "This Act shall not be construed as permitting the censorship of any film or film-publicity material published, displayed, circulated, exhibited, distributed or transmitted over the internet or over intranets." The claim appears in the scenario called "post a set online". Telling an artist that a film they posted online needs Board approval is not a nuance error; it is advice to submit to a censorship regime that the statute expressly disclaims over that medium, and it is the exact mirror of s.3(3) of the Communications and Multimedia Act, which the site does not cite anywhere and should (see Material Omissions). The Board's practice does read "film" widely, and Board approval is the gate for physical exhibition, distribution and possession, so the sentence is right about everything except the one medium the scenario is about. Note as well s.2(2)(d), which takes a film that is not obscene or lewd out of the Act altogether while it is held for the possessor's own private use.
- REPLACE WITH: "The Film Censorship Act does not apply to what you put on the internet. Section 2(3) says the Act is not to be construed as permitting the censorship of any film transmitted over the internet or an intranet. The moment the same work moves offline, into a screening, a gallery, a festival or physical copies, Board approval becomes the gate and the Board reads 'film' widely enough to catch shorts, music videos and festival material. Online, your exposure is s.233 and the Online Safety Act, not the Board."

### [mural#04], [publish-book#05], [stage-show#12] and [arrested#05]: s.298A

- WRONG: every claim that cites s.298A of the Penal Code as live law without qualification, for example "the obscenity provisions and s.298A on religious disharmony are the two that reach public imagery".
- WHY: Section 298A was held unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in *Mamat bin Daud & Ors v Government of Malaysia* [1988] 1 MLJ 119. The majority held that in pith and substance it is a law with respect to the religion of Islam, which is a State matter under the State List of the Ninth Schedule, so Parliament had no competence to enact it for the States; it was described as colourable legislation. It remains printed in the Penal Code and it is still charged, and it has never been formally repealed, so an artist can absolutely be arrested under it. But a resource for artists that names s.298A as a risk and does not tell the reader that the highest court has held it beyond Parliament's power is withholding the first thing their lawyer will raise. The other feature the claims omit is that s.298A(1) carries a **mandatory minimum of two years' imprisonment** and s.298A(2) disapplies ss.173A and 294 of the Criminal Procedure Code, so a discharge or a bond for good behaviour is not available. That is the difference between a caution and a prison sentence and it belongs on the page.
- REPLACE WITH: "Section 298A on causing religious disharmony also reaches public imagery, and it is the heaviest of these: a minimum of two years' imprisonment, with the Criminal Procedure Code's discharge and good-behaviour-bond routes expressly shut off. Raise its constitutionality early. The Supreme Court held in Mamat bin Daud v Government of Malaysia [1988] 1 MLJ 119 that s.298A is in substance a law about religion, which is a State matter, and so beyond Parliament's power. It has never been repealed and it is still charged. Note the limit of the argument: the reasoning turns on Parliament legislating for the States, so it is at its strongest outside the Federal Territories and weakest in Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan, where Parliament does legislate on State subjects."
- NOTE FOR THE EDITOR: ALIM's readership is concentrated in Kuala Lumpur, which is where the *Mamat bin Daud* argument is at its weakest. Do not let the replacement sentence be cut down to the first half, because a KL artist told flatly that s.298A is unconstitutional will rely on it and be wrong.

### [dj-club-night#13] and [busking#03] and [stage-show#12]

- WRONG: "Section 294 reaches an obscene song, ballad or words in or near a public place."
- WHY: Incomplete in a way that matters to a defence. Section 294 of the Penal Code opens with the words "Whoever, to the annoyance of others". Annoyance to others is an element the prosecution must establish; it is not a garnish. A DJ or performer reading this page should know that the absence of any annoyed person is the first line of defence, and that the maximum is three months' imprisonment or a fine, which puts it in a different bracket from everything else on the page. Leaving out the element is the difference between an artist who knows they have a defence and one who pleads.
- REPLACE WITH: "Section 294(b) reaches whoever, **to the annoyance of others**, sings, recites or utters any obscene song, ballad or words in or near any public place. Annoyance to somebody is part of the offence, not background, and it is the first thing to test. The maximum is three months' imprisonment, a fine, or both."

### [busking#02] and [mural#05]

- WRONG: "Begging and public nuisance offences, read with those by-laws, are how street performance is actually policed" / "Public nuisance, read with the by-law, is the everyday enforcement route."
- WHY: The hosted file cannot be checked at all: `legislation/pdf/act-336-bi.pdf` is not the English text of the Minor Offences Act 1955. It is a scanned image of the **Malay** text, "Akta Kesalahan Kecil 1955 (Disemak—1987)", with no text layer, and its own title page carries the standard notice that the text is only a translation by the Attorney General's Chambers and that, until prescribed as authoritative under s.7(1) of the National Language Act 1963/67, "ia bukannya perundangan": it is not legislation. I therefore checked the claims against the English reprint published by the Ministry of Home Affairs, and the answer to the question the brief asks is as follows.

  **The begging framing is accurate, and it is in fact sharper than the claim states.** Section 27(c) deems to be an idle and disorderly person "every person begging in a public place, or in a place to which the public are admitted without payment or on payment, or using in any such place conduct calculated to induce the giving of alms **(whether or not there is any pretence of singing, playing, performing, offering anything for sale or otherwise)**, and having or able to have other lawful means of livelihood". The statute expressly contemplates that singing, playing and performing may be treated as a pretence for soliciting alms. The penalty is a fine not exceeding RM100 or imprisonment not exceeding one month or both. Two things follow that the claim should carry: the closing words, "having or able to have other lawful means of livelihood", are an element, so a busker with other means is outside the provision, and that is the defence; and s.27(a), unlicensed hawkers, is the companion risk the moment the busker sells a CD or a print.

  **The public nuisance framing is the weaker half and should be replaced with the right provisions.** For busking, the operative section is **s.13, excessive noise**: s.13(1)(a) catches beating a drum or sounding any instrument in any public road at any hour so as to cause annoyance or inconvenience to occupants of premises in the vicinity, and s.13(1)(c) catches operating any instrument for the production of noise or music in or near a public road so as to cause the same, each punishable by a fine not exceeding RM100. Section 13(3) is the practical escape: nothing in the section applies to anything done with the permission of the Chief Police Officer. For murals, the provision is not public nuisance at all; it is **s.15(2)**, affixing bills or otherwise defacing property, which catches a person who without authority in the case of public property, or without the owner's or occupier's consent in the case of private property, "writes upon, marks, defaces or tampers with chalk or paint or in any other way any property", where "property" is defined to include any building, wall, road, fence, tree, lamp standard, lamp or telephone post, traffic sign and notice board. That carries **imprisonment up to one year or a fine up to RM1,000 or both**, an order of magnitude above the RM100 minor offences, and it is the real exposure for an unpermitted mural. Section 14, insulting behaviour, adds a further RM100 offence for exhibiting or affixing an indecent, threatening, abusive or insulting drawing with intent to provoke a breach of the peace, or where a breach of the peace is likely.

  **And the territorial limit, which is missing from both claims and from the library.** Section 1(2) reads: "This Act shall apply to Peninsular Malaysia only." Every Act 336 claim on this site is inapplicable in Sabah and Sarawak. Section 33 then lets a State Legislative Assembly legislate on minor criminal matters prescribed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, capped at RM100 or three months, which is part of why the position differs by State.
- REPLACE WITH (busking#02): "The Minor Offences Act 1955 applies in Peninsular Malaysia only. Two provisions do the work. Section 13 on excessive noise catches sounding an instrument, or operating anything that produces noise or music, in or near a public road so as to annoy or inconvenience people in nearby premises; the fine is up to RM100, and s.13(3) exempts anything done with the permission of the Chief Police Officer, so that permission is worth having. Section 27(c) deems a person an idle and disorderly person for begging or for conduct calculated to induce the giving of alms, and it says in terms that it applies whether or not there is any pretence of singing, playing or performing. Whether you are performing or soliciting alms is the whole question and it gets decided on the street. Note the last words of s.27(c): it only bites on someone 'having or able to have other lawful means of livelihood', and that is your answer."
- REPLACE WITH (mural#05): "In Peninsular Malaysia the everyday enforcement route is s.15(2) of the Minor Offences Act 1955, affixing bills or otherwise defacing property, which catches writing upon, marking or defacing any property with chalk or paint or in any other way without authority for public property or the owner's or occupier's consent for private property. 'Property' expressly includes a building, wall, road, fence, tree, lamp post, traffic sign or notice board. The penalty is up to one year's imprisonment or a fine up to RM1,000 or both, so it is heavier than it sounds. Section 14 adds a lesser offence where an exhibited drawing is indecent, threatening, abusive or insulting and a breach of the peace is intended or likely. The Act does not apply in Sabah or Sarawak."

  The hosted file must still be replaced before either claim is published, because a reader who follows the link cannot check a word of this.

### [post-set-online#04] and [brand-deal#02]

- WRONG: "Registered under s.213 of the Communications and Multimedia Act, so compliance is a defence" / "compliance with it is a defence under s.213 of the Communications and Multimedia Act"
- WHY: Wrong section, and the mechanism is misdescribed. Section 213 does not register anything; it says only what a content code prepared by the content forum or the Commission "shall include" and "may address". The chain is: the Commission designates the Communications and Multimedia Content Forum of Malaysia as the content forum under s.212, read with the industry forum provisions in s.94; the code is prepared under s.95(1) and s.213; it is **registered by the Commission under s.95**, and s.95(2) says it is not effective until registered. ## FLAGGED BUT DEFENSIBLE

### [post-set-online#05] Online Safety Act 2025

"In force from 1 January 2026" is **correct**. Act 866 received Royal Assent on 6 May 2025, was gazetted on 22 May 2025, s.1(2) left commencement to the Minister, and the Minister appointed 1 January 2026; MCMC began enforcing against platforms from that date. The framing that the exposure is removal by a platform rather than a charge is also correct and well judged: ss.13 to 20 place the duties on licensed applications service providers, content applications service providers and network service providers, and the Act does not create offences committed by ordinary users.

The risk is in the last clause. "A removal is far harder to appeal than a charge is to defend" is presented as though there were no route. There is a route, and a reader should be told where it is. Sections 21 to 26 give a user who is aggrieved by a report being dismissed, or by the action the provider took, the right to make an inquiry and to be answered in writing within the prescribed period. Part VII establishes the **Online Safety Appeal Tribunal**, and s.41 lets a person aggrieved by a Commission written instruction under ss.23(8), 25(1) or 27, a determination under s.25(5), directions under s.30 or a review decision under s.38 appeal to it; s.41(2) lets the Tribunal confirm or set aside. Section 70 then preserves judicial review in the High Court, subject to s.70(2), which requires the applicant to exhaust the remedies under the Act first. That last point is the practical one and the page should carry it: **go to the Tribunal first or you will lose the judicial review on exhaustion**.

Suggested addition rather than a rewrite: "There is a route. Sections 21 to 26 give you an inquiry and a written answer from the platform. Part VII sets up an Online Safety Appeal Tribunal and s.41 says who may appeal to it. Section 70 preserves judicial review in the High Court, but s.70(2) requires you to exhaust the remedies in the Act first, so go to the Tribunal before you go to court."

### [documentary#01] Film Censorship Act

The s.6 point is **correct** and well made: s.6(1)(a) catches a person who has an unapproved film "in his possession or in his custody or under his control", quite apart from exhibition. Penalty on conviction in respect of a film is a fine of not less than RM5,000 and not more than RM30,000 or up to three years or both, so there is a mandatory minimum fine. Two gaps.

First, the possession point needs its exception or it overstates. Section 2(2)(d) takes out of the Act altogether "any film, not being obscene or lewd, which is in the possession of any person or his agent and is intended for his own private use and not for the purpose of sale, hire, distribution, public exhibition or circulation so long as the film remains in such possession". A filmmaker holding her own rushes at home is not committing an offence, and the page as written implies she is. Section 5 is the separate and heavier provision, obscene or indecent film, with a fine of RM10,000 to RM50,000 or up to five years.

Second, the claim says "the Appeal Committee is the first real remedy" and gives no section numbers at all. They are: **s.21** appeal by the owner within thirty days of notification, on payment of the prescribed fee, by written notice lodged with the Secretary; **s.22** the composition of the Appeal Committee, whose members under s.22(1) are a Chairman and Vice-Chairman appointed by the Minister, the Inspector General of Police or his representative, three ministry or department heads or their representatives, and thirteen further members appointed by the Minister; **s.23(1)** its power to confirm, vary or reverse. And then the part that should not be omitted from a sentence recommending the remedy: **s.23(2)** says its decision "shall be final and shall not be questioned in any court of law", and **s.48** says no decision of the Minister, the Board or the Appeal Committee is subject to appeal or review by any court on any ground. Recommending the Appeal Committee is right precisely because the statute tries to make it the last word. Say so.

### [documentary#02] FINAS Act

Substantively right, sections missing, and one strong point left on the table.

The duty is **s.22(1)**: no person shall engage in production, distribution or exhibition of films, or any combination specified in s.21(1), without a licence. The offence is **s.25(1)**, contravention of any provision of that Part, punishable by a fine up to RM50,000 or two years or both, plus a daily fine up to RM10,000 for a continuing offence, and s.26 deems every director, secretary, manager or partner guilty as well unless they prove absence of knowledge, consent or connivance and due diligence. The provision an independent documentary maker should fear most is **s.24B**: anyone in possession, custody or control of three or more copies of a film of the same title is presumed, unless the contrary is proved, to be engaged in distribution. Three DVDs in a bag reverses the burden of proof.

The point worth adding is the contrast with the Printing Presses and Publications Act that the site makes elsewhere: **s.22(3)** says FINAS may refuse a licence without assigning any reason and its decision "shall be final and conclusive and shall not be challenged, appealed against, reviewed, quashed or questioned in any court", and s.25(2) applies the same ouster to cancellation. That is a full ouster clause, still on the books, and it is a large part of why the Act is called the most-criticised barrier to entry. Say it; the scenario is weaker without it.

One caution on "this is the provision that has been used against independent documentary makers". The most-cited prosecution of a Malaysian documentary screening, that of Lena Hendry over *No Fire Zone*, was brought under **s.6(1)(b) of the Film Censorship Act**, not the FINAS Act. FINAS licensing has been used investigatively against independent producers and festivals, but if the sentence is meant to evoke that case it is pointing at the wrong statute. Either name the FINAS matter you mean or soften to "has been used against independent film-makers and festivals".

### [dj-club-night#03], [stage-show#03], [foreign-artist#04], [arrested#02] Entertainment (FT of KL) Act

Correct, and incomplete in ways that cost a promoter money.

There are **two** licences, not one. Section 4 requires an entertainment licence, and s.5(4) says no entertainment licence may be granted unless a licence to open the place of entertainment has already been obtained. A promoter who has the venue's premises licence does not thereby have the entertainment licence for the night.

The content control is sharper than "the conditions attached to it". It is **s.6(1)**, which lets the Commissioner require the applicant to furnish "the script, if any", and s.6(2) defines script to include the text of a play with stage directions, "whether expressed in words or in musical or other notation". Prior submission of the script is the mechanism. For a theatre producer that is the single most important sentence in the Act and it is not on the page.

The claims say twice that the remedy is different because it is licence-based, and never name it. It is **s.12**: appeal to the Minister within thirty days of written notice of a refusal, revocation, imposition of conditions or restrictions, or forfeiture of security. The sting, which must go on the page with it, is that s.12 ends "the decision of the Minister shall be final and shall not be questioned in any court". Also relevant: s.9, a licence is not renewable as of right and a renewal is treated as a fresh application; s.11, the Commissioner may revoke at any time for breach of a condition; s.17, power of closure.

And the trap nobody warns promoters about: **s.13** makes it unlawful to give away, distribute, sell or offer any ticket, or to advertise or publicise the entertainment in any manner, before the s.4 licence has been granted. Pre-sales before the licence lands are an offence. That belongs in the DJ and stage-show scenarios.

### [busking#01], [dj-club-night#05], [mural#01], [arrested#03] Local Government Act

Correct, and improvable by naming the paragraph. The general by-law power is **s.102**, and the relevant limbs are **s.102(j)**, to "regulate, license, supervise, restrict or prohibit the playing of musical instruments, singing or performing for profit, in any public place", which is the busking power exactly; **s.102(p)**, establishment, regulation, inspection and licensing of places of entertainment, public recreation or public resort; and **s.102(s)**, control of a trade or business that could be a source of nuisance. Naming s.102(j) turns a general statement into something a busker can quote to an officer.

One qualification to busking#01's "publish separately, so there is no national answer and none of it is on the federal portal". True and well said about the federal portal, but **s.103** requires a by-law to be confirmed by the State Authority and **s.106** provides that publication in the Gazette constitutes notice. So the by-law is a published instrument with a citation, and an artist is entitled to ask the officer for it. The honest version of the claim is stronger, not weaker, for saying that.

### [publish-book#04] and [arrested#06] Sedition Act

**Verified correct.** Section 3(3) of the Sedition Act 1948 provides that the intention of the person charged is "deemed to be irrelevant if in fact the act had, or would, if done, have had, or the words, publication or thing had a seditious tendency". The claim's gloss, that this is what makes the Act dangerous to satire, is sound.

The flag is the hosted consolidation, which is as at 1 January 2006, and here I have to be careful in a way the page must be too. The Sedition (Amendment) Act 2015 (Act A1485) was passed by Parliament in April 2015 and gazetted. It would remove the limb about exciting disaffection against the administration of justice, add a religious-hatred limb, raise penalties and add powers over electronic publication. **I could not confirm that it was ever brought into operation.** Its commencement was left to the Minister by notification in the Gazette; secondary sources conflict, and several state that the amendments have never been brought into force. I am not prepared to assert either way on this record, and neither should ALIM.

That uncertainty is itself the finding. Until someone checks the gazette directly, the page should not claim the 2015 amendments are in force and should not claim they are not. What it can safely say is that the hosted text is a 2006 consolidation, that Parliament passed amendments in 2015 whose commencement should be checked before relying on section numbering or penalties, and that the Federal Court has upheld the constitutionality of s.4(1) in *Public Prosecutor v Azmi bin Sharom* (Federal Court, 2015). I have not verified that report citation and it should be checked against the law reports before it is printed; the case name, court and year are safe. None of this affects the s.3(3) claim, which is correct.

### [documentary#05], [arrested#11], [archive#08] Official Secrets Act

**Verified correct on both limbs.** The mandatory custodial element is real: s.8(1) and s.8(2) are each "punishable with imprisonment for a term not less than one year but not exceeding seven years", s.4(1) not less than one year and not exceeding fourteen, ss.7A(2) and 7B not less than one year and not exceeding five. There is no fine-only option. And s.16A provides that a certificate by a Minister, a public officer charged with responsibility for a ministry, department or public service, a Menteri Besar, a Chief Minister or a State's principal administrative officer certifying that a document, information or material is an official secret "shall be conclusive evidence" and "shall not be questioned in any court on any ground whatsoever".

The flag is the inference the claim draws, that "there is no route to argue the material was innocuous". As to classification, that is right. But the conclusiveness attaches to the fact of classification, not to every ingredient of every offence, and the certificate does not prove the accused's conduct. Defence counsel should not read this page as saying there is nothing to run. Suggest softening to "there is no route to argue the material should not have been classified" rather than "was innocuous".

### [post-set-online#09], [busking#07], [mural#07], [arrested#01] Federal Constitution

Correct as far as they go, and one point wants tightening. post-set-online#09 says "Article 10(2)(a) is the power they are made under". Accurate for speech restrictions, but the Acts on these pages are not all made under Article 10(2)(a): the Peaceful Assembly Act is Article 10(2)(b) and 10(4); the Sedition Act limbs on Part III and Articles 152, 153 and 181 engage Article 10(4) specifically; the Printing Presses and Publications Act licensing regime is as much a Ninth Schedule competence question as an Article 10 one. Suggest "Article 10(2), and for assembly Article 10(2)(b) and 10(4)".

arrested#01's pairing of Article 5 with Article 10 is right and should be built out, not trimmed. See Material Omissions.

### [dj-club-night#04], [busking#06], [stage-show#04], [foreign-artist#05] State enactments

The constitutional basis is **verified and should be stated on the page**, because it is what makes the claim checkable. Ninth Schedule, List II, item 5 opens "Except with respect to the Federal Territories of Kuala Lumpur, Labuan and Putrajaya, other services of a local character", and item 5(f) is "Licensing of theatres, cinemas and places of public amusement". That carve-out for the Federal Territories is precisely why the Entertainment (Federal Territory of Kuala Lumpur) Act 1992 is a federal statute, and it is the cleanest way to explain to an artist why KL is different.

One precision the page should not fudge: it is the **licensing** that is on the State List. The subject matter itself sits on the Federal List, item 23, "Subject to item 5(f) of the State List; theatres; cinemas; cinematograph films; places of public amusement", and item 22 is "Censorship", which is wholly federal. Sabah and Sarawak are different again: in List IIA, the Supplement to the State List for those States, item 16 is "Theatres; cinemas; cinematograph films; places of public amusement" without the limitation to licensing. So saying flatly that "entertainment licensing is on the State List" is right; saying entertainment is a State subject would not be, except in Sabah and Sarawak.

foreign-artist#05's separate Sabah and Sarawak immigration control is correct; the basis is Part VII of the Immigration Act 1959/63 and Article 161E of the Federal Constitution, and a work pass valid for the peninsula does not carry into either State.

### [arrested#10] Syariah criminal enactments

Directionally right and the most dangerous claim on the page to leave at one sentence, because it is the one where an artist who relies on it will be most surprised. It is right that a State Syariah criminal enactment runs in parallel for Muslims and that federal clearance is no answer to a State charge. Three things should be added or the sentence should be shortened until they can be.

First, the jurisdictional ceiling. State Syariah courts are limited by the Syariah Courts (Criminal Jurisdiction) Act 1965 (Act 355) to three years' imprisonment, a RM5,000 fine and six strokes. That is the single most useful fact for an artist told they face a Syariah charge.

Second, the competence limit. Under Ninth Schedule List II item 1 the State may only create offences "against precepts of the religion of Islam" by persons professing that religion, and only "except in regard to matters included in the Federal List". Where a State enactment duplicates a federal offence it is open to challenge; that is the line of reasoning in *Iki Putra bin Mubarrak v Kerajaan Negeri Selangor* [2021] 2 MLJ 323, in which the Federal Court struck down a State Syariah provision as beyond State competence because Parliament had already legislated on the subject.

Third, and most practically, the enactments differ State by State and the relevant offences for artists are typically the ones on insulting Islam, deriding religious precepts, indecent behaviour and, in several States, performance or dress. A single sentence saying "it runs in parallel" does not let anyone act.

## MATERIAL OMISSIONS

**1. Arrest, police questioning and the right to counsel. This is the biggest hole in the resource and it is in the scenario called "arrested".**

All eleven claims in the `arrested` scenario describe the statutes an artist might be charged under. Not one tells them what happens in the first twenty-four hours, which is the only part they can actually influence. There is no claim on the Criminal Procedure Code at all, anywhere in the 178. An artist reading this page learns which Act they may be prosecuted under and nothing about the moment that decides the case. The following should be a claim of its own, ahead of everything else in the scenario:

- **Article 5(3) of the Federal Constitution**: a person arrested shall be informed as soon as may be of the grounds of arrest and **shall be allowed to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of his choice**.
- **Article 5(4)**: no detention beyond twenty-four hours without being produced before a magistrate, excluding time necessary for the journey.
- **Section 28A of the Criminal Procedure Code**: the operative provision. The police must inform the arrested person of the grounds of arrest; must inform them that they may communicate with, or attempt to communicate with, a relative or friend and a legal practitioner of their choice; and, where a lawyer is asked for, must allow a reasonable time for that lawyer to attend at the place of detention and for the consultation to take place, before questioning or recording a statement. **The warning that must travel with this, and which I would not publish s.28A without, is s.28A(8): the requirements in subsections (2) to (7) do not apply where the officer reasonably believes that compliance is likely to result in an accomplice avoiding apprehension, in the concealment, fabrication or destruction of evidence or the intimidation of a witness, or where, having regard to the safety of other persons, the questioning is so urgent that it should not be delayed.** That is a lawful power to delay access to counsel, not a technicality, and an artist who has been told they have an absolute right to a lawyer and is then refused one will conclude the police are acting illegally when they may not be. Publish both halves. I have taken this from secondary sources and it should be checked against the section text before it is printed.
- **Section 117 of the Criminal Procedure Code**: remand. This is where an artist is actually harmed, by days in custody with no charge ever filed, and it is the application a lawyer can resist.
- **The right to silence** and the practical point that what is said in an unrepresented interview is usually the entire prosecution case in a s.233 or Sedition Act file.
- **Bail**: whether an offence is bailable is set out in the First Schedule to the Criminal Procedure Code and it should be checked there for each offence rather than assumed. The point worth making on the page is that the offences catalogued in these scenarios are not uniform, and the mandatory-minimum provisions, s.298A of the Penal Code and the Official Secrets Act offences, are where an artist should expect the position to be worst.

This is squarely within a law NGO's remit and it is the single most useful thing ALIM could publish for artists. Publishing the catalogue of offences without it is close to irresponsible.

**1A. Section 3(3) of the Communications and Multimedia Act, which the site does not cite anywhere.** Section 3(3) of Act 588 reads: "Nothing in this Act shall be construed as permitting the censorship of the Internet." No claim in my scope invokes it, which is a missed opportunity, because it is the artist's opening argument in any MCMC matter and it is the direct analogue of s.2(3) of the Film Censorship Act. It should be handled honestly rather than as a slogan: s.3(3) has not prevented a single s.233 prosecution, and the tension between the two provisions has never been resolved by an appellate court, with the Federal Court in *Heidy Quah* deciding the point on Article 10(1)(a) rather than on s.3(3). Say both things. A statutory declaration against internet censorship sitting in the same Act as the provision most used to prosecute internet speech is exactly the kind of thing a legal-information resource for artists exists to point at.


**2. Seizure of equipment and work.** Every one of these statutes carries a seizure power, and for an artist the loss of the instrument, the camera, the hard drive or the only copy of the work is the real penalty, usually long before any trial. Film Censorship Act ss.32 to 36 and s.40 forfeiture, with s.42 providing that no costs or damages arising from a seizure can be recovered; FINAS Act Part VA; Printing Presses and Publications Act s.3(5), under which an unlicensed printing press is forfeited by order of the court **even if nobody is convicted of any offence**; Entertainment (FT of KL) Act ss.21 to 24, including s.24, under which things seized where there is no prosecution are deemed forfeited if not claimed within one month. That one-month clock is a trap and nothing on the site mentions it.

**3. Penal Code s.504.** It is in the review brief and in the Act, but it does not appear in any claim. Intentional insult with intent to provoke a breach of the peace, up to two years, is a routine add-on charge alongside s.233 and s.298A in exactly the fact patterns these scenarios describe. It should be listed with ss.298 and 505 in `arrested#05`.

**4. Computer Crimes Act 1997 (Act 563).** The Act is hosted at `legislation/pdf/act-563-bi.pdf` but appears in `by-instrument.txt` against no claim at all and in none of the 178 claims. It is squarely relevant twice over: an artist whose account, site or archive is broken into needs s.3 (unauthorised access), s.4 (unauthorised access with intent to commit a further offence) and s.5 (unauthorised modification of contents), and those are also the provisions under which an artist doing adversarial or data-driven work can themselves be charged, with s.8 reversing the burden of proof in defined circumstances. Either give it a claim in `someone-used-my-work` and `arrested`, or remove it from the library so the omission is not read as a considered exclusion.

**5. Sabah and Sarawak generally.** Only `foreign-artist#05` mentions that they are different. In this area they are different in more ways than immigration: List IIA item 16 puts theatres, cinemas, cinematograph films and places of public amusement wholly on the State List for both, so a Sabahan or Sarawakian artist reading the entertainment-licensing pages is reading about a constitutional position that does not apply to them in the same terms. A standing note in the state-entertainment claims would fix it.

**6. The Printing Presses and Publications Act still contains s.8A, false news.** Malicious publication of false news, up to three years or RM20,000 or both, with **malice presumed** under s.8A(2) unless the accused shows they took reasonable measures to verify before publishing, and prosecution requiring the Public Prosecutor's written consent under s.8A(3). For a zine, a memoir or an investigative catalogue essay, a presumption of malice is a more serious practical exposure than the s.7 prohibition power the scenario leads on, and nothing on the page mentions it.
